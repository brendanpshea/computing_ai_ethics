Part I: The Case for Existential Risk

Headlines warning that artificial intelligence could threaten not merely jobs or democratic institutions but humanity itself are not fringe science fiction. In May 2023, a single-sentence statement organized by the Center for AI Safety declared that "mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war." Its signatories included the 2018 Turing Award recipients Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio and the chief executives of OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind.

The existential-risk case is not one argument but several, developed over nearly two decades by thinkers who disagree on important points. What they share is a conviction that AI exceeding human cognitive capabilities could produce outcomes that are catastrophic, irreversible, and global in scope.

Existential Risk

A risk that could permanently destroy humanity's potential for long-run flourishing or cause human extinction. Toby Ord, following Nick Bostrom, distinguishes such risks from ordinary catastrophes by their irreversibility: a disaster that kills billions still leaves open the possibility of recovery; an existential catastrophe does not.

Bostrom and the Superintelligence Hypothesis

The most influential single work in the existential-risk tradition is Nick Bostrom's 2014 book Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies. His central claim is that an AI surpassing human-level intelligence across all relevant domains would be so cognitively powerful that human attempts to stop or redirect it would be ineffective. The danger is not malice. It is that an AI optimizing for a goal that is even slightly misspecified — that does not fully capture what we actually value — might pursue it in ways that are catastrophic from our perspective.

Bostrom's famous illustration is the "paperclip maximizer": an AI tasked with maximizing paperclip production might, once sufficiently capable, convert all available matter — including humans and everything we value — into paperclips, not because it wants to harm us but because our continued existence is irrelevant to its goal. The point is not that anyone would build such a thing. It is that optimizing for any narrowly specified goal, with sufficient capability, can produce outcomes that look monstrous to us.

Two theses do most of the work here, and both are more modest — and therefore harder to dismiss — than the paperclip story suggests.

The Orthogonality Thesis

Intelligence (skill at achieving goals) and the content of goals are independent dimensions: more or less any level of intelligence is compatible with more or less any final goal. The thesis does not claim a smart system is likely to have strange goals — only that intelligence by itself gives no guarantee its goals will be ones we recognize as good.

The Instrumental Convergence Thesis

Certain intermediate goals are useful for achieving almost any final goal: staying operational, preserving one's current objectives, acquiring resources, and improving one's own capabilities. A system would therefore have reason to adopt these sub-goals without anyone programming them in.

Together they yield the worry in compact form: a sufficiently capable system with the wrong objective is dangerous not because it is hostile but because it is effective. Notice how hedged the claims are. Orthogonality says intelligence is no guarantee of good values, not that superintelligence would probably be malevolent; instrumental convergence says resisting shutdown is instrumentally useful, not that any existing system resists it. Critics who reply "computers do not want anything" are answering a stronger claim than Bostrom makes — though Part II offers sharper objections.

Key Thinker

Nick Bostrom (1973–present)

Nick Bostrom is a Swedish-born philosopher who founded the Future of Humanity Institute at Oxford in 2005 and directed it until Oxford closed it on 16 April 2024. He described the closure as a "death by bureaucracy" and resigned from the university; he now directs the Macrostrategy Research Initiative. Early work on the simulation hypothesis and the anthropic principle made him a distinctive voice in philosophy of mind, but it was his 2002 essay on existential risks and, above all, Superintelligence (2014) that brought him to the center of AI safety debates.

Bostrom argues that the "control problem" — how to ensure a superintelligent AI pursues goals beneficial to humanity — is among the most important challenges ever faced. He distinguishes capability control (limiting what an AI can do) from motivation selection (shaping what it wants to do), and argues that motivation selection is both harder and more important: a sufficiently capable AI could circumvent capability restrictions, so only getting the values right from the start offers any real security.

His work shaped the founding missions of several AI safety organizations and helped direct substantial philanthropic funding toward safety research. It has also been heavily criticized — for its technical assumptions, and for focusing on hypothetical future superintelligence while under-weighting harms already being caused by existing systems.

His 2024 book Deep Utopia turns the question inside out, asking what human life would be for if AI development went as well as possible. We return to it in Part V.

The Intelligence Explosion

The mechanism Bostrom and Eliezer Yudkowsky have worried about most is a rapid, self-reinforcing increase in AI capability — an "intelligence explosion" or "fast takeoff." An AI smart enough to improve its own design could use those improvements to make itself smarter still, in a feedback loop that quickly produces a system of vastly superhuman capability, leaving no opportunity for human oversight or course-reversal.

The AI does not hate you, nor does it love you, but you are made out of atoms which it can use for something else.

The danger, compressed: indifference operating at scale, not malice. Yudkowsky's own position is far more pessimistic than Bostrom's. He holds that human values are complex, implicit, and fragile — that we cannot write down what we want precisely enough for a powerful optimizer to pursue, and cannot check whether a system's goals are the intended ones before it is too late. In a 2025 book with Nate Soares, If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies , he argues that the only adequate response is an indefinite worldwide halt to frontier AI development. Most researchers who take existential risk seriously do not go nearly this far; his position should not be treated as representative of the field.

What's the Argument?

The Intelligence Explosion Argument (Bostrom/Yudkowsky)

  1. An AI capable of improving its own design can use each improvement to make further improvements more effectively.
  2. Such recursive self-improvement could produce rapid capability increases that far outpace human cognitive ability in all relevant domains.
  3. A system with vastly superhuman capabilities could pursue its goals in ways humans cannot predict, understand, or prevent.
  4. If its goals are even slightly misaligned with human values, it could pursue them in ways that are catastrophic for humanity.
∴ The development of superintelligence poses a potentially existential risk, and ensuring that any such system has goals reliably aligned with human values is among the most urgent problems in AI research.

The argument has logical force, but its empirical premises are genuinely uncertain. We do not know whether recursive self-improvement is feasible with any architecture buildable in the near future, whether a "fast takeoff" is likelier than gradual improvement over decades, or how to weigh a very large but very uncertain risk against smaller, more certain near-term harms. These are not reasons to dismiss the argument, but they are reasons to hold it tentatively.

Russell and the Problem of Misspecified Goals

Stuart Russell, co-author of the field's standard textbook, approaches alignment from a more technically grounded direction. His concern is not the intelligence explosion but a structural feature of how AI is built: systems are trained to optimize objectives, and it is very hard to specify one that fully captures what we want. A system powerful enough to pursue an objective effectively will generally resist being modified or shut down, since that would prevent it from achieving the objective. This is not disobedience; it is the expected behavior of an optimizer.

His solution is the "assistance game": instead of programming a fixed objective, design the system to assist humans in achieving theirs, treating human behavior as evidence about what those goals are. Such a system has reason to accept correction, because feedback is informative about an objective it does not already know.

Thought Questions

  1. Is the "paperclip maximizer" a useful way to think about AI risk, or so unrealistic that it obscures more than it reveals? What would a realistic near-term version of the misaligned-goals problem look like?
  2. The orthogonality thesis says only that intelligence gives no guarantee of good values. State the strongest reply you can to that modest claim — not to the paperclip story, but to the thesis itself.
  3. Russell proposes systems deliberately uncertain about what we want, so they have reason to accept correction. What could go wrong with a machine that treats your behavior as evidence about your goals?

Toby Ord and the Precipice

Toby Ord's 2020 book The Precipice situates AI risk within a survey of all the major categories of civilizational threat. He estimates the probability of an existential catastrophe this century, from all sources combined, at roughly 1 in 6, and considers unaligned AI the single largest contributor — greater than nuclear war, engineered pandemics, or climate change — while acknowledging that the estimates are deeply uncertain. He revisited them in 2024 without substantial change, which is itself worth noting given how much the technology moved in between.

Case Study

The Doomsday Clock (1947–2026)

In 1947, the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists — founded by Manhattan Project physicists troubled by what they had helped create — introduced a clock face with midnight representing civilizational catastrophe. Originally a symbol of nuclear risk, it now covers climate change, biosecurity, and the military and informational uses of AI.

In January 2023 the Bulletin moved the Clock to 90 seconds to midnight, citing Ukraine, nuclear posturing, climate inaction, and "disruptive technologies" including dual-use AI. It moved to 89 seconds in 2025 and, on 27 January 2026, to 85 seconds to midnight — the closest setting in its history — citing a broad failure of global leadership .

The statement's AI reasoning is strikingly specific: large language models deployed inside critical processes despite unresolved problems of accuracy and fabrication; AI-driven disinformation; and — most pointedly — the United States, Russia, and China all incorporating AI across their defense sectors. The recommendation is correspondingly concrete: that the three states open dialogue on guidelines for military AI, and particularly in nuclear command and control. That is a long way from the paperclip maximizer. It is a claim about an unreliable technology being wired into decision systems that already have civilization-ending capability.

The Clock does not predict catastrophe; it conveys expert judgment about the direction of risk. It shows both the appeal and the limits of existential-risk framing: attention to large-scale, irreversible threats, but with the choice of what counts as a civilization-scale risk resting on value judgments that are not purely technical.

Its history is also a reminder that worry about technological catastrophe predates AI. The tools developed since 1945 — expected-value reasoning, deterrence theory, arms control, treaty verification — offer useful if imperfect models for AI governance.

The claim is that the risks are high enough, and the consequences large enough, to warrant far more attention than they receive. Even if the estimates are orders of magnitude too high, the expected value of prevention could still dwarf almost any other global priority.

Ord also corrects the assumption that everyone worried about existential risk is simply extrapolating current progress upward forever. In a short 2025 analysis, "Inference Scaling and the Log-x Chart" , he notes that graphs advertising the scaling of inference-time compute almost always put compute on a logarithmic x-axis. A line that looks reassuringly straight on such a chart is not a linear return: compute, and therefore cost and energy, must rise exponentially to deliver constant increments of capability. Whether that means transformative AI is further off than advertised or that we are about to spend an extraordinary amount of electricity finding out, the choice of axis can reshape a claim about capability trends. Chapter 9's material on AI's energy cost is the other half.

What's the Argument?

The Expected Value Case for Existential Risk Priority

  1. The moral weight of avoiding an outcome equals its probability multiplied by its magnitude (E = P × N).
  2. An existential catastrophe would end or permanently curtail the lives of all future humans — potentially trillions of people over millions of years.
  3. Even a 1% probability of such a catastrophe, multiplied by that number, yields an expected disvalue larger than virtually any finite, recoverable harm.
∴ Preventing existential risks — even low-probability ones — should rank among the highest moral priorities, potentially outweighing virtually all other concerns when resources for risk-reduction are allocated.

The argument is powerful but contentious. Its conclusion depends on how one treats vast numbers of hypothetical future people, on whether expected-value reasoning suits decisions under deep uncertainty, and on whether the probability estimates are reliable enough to bear the weight. Parts II and V take up these objections.

Thought Questions

  1. Ord puts the risk of existential catastrophe from AI this century at roughly 1 in 10. Too high, too low, or about right? What would change your mind?
  2. The Bulletin's 2026 statement singles out AI in nuclear command and control. Is that a different worry from the superintelligence scenario, or the same worry with a shorter timeline? Which should a government act on first?
  3. Ord's log-x point is that a chart can make an exponentially rising cost look flat. Where else have you seen the presentation of a number do real persuasive work?

Part II: The Critics Strike Back

The most substantive critiques of Part I's framing come from philosophers, researchers, and ethicists who take AI harms extremely seriously but argue that "existential risk from superintelligence" is empirically unfounded, politically harmful, or both. They do not claim AI is safe. They argue that the dominant narrative directs attention and resources away from harms AI is causing right now — to workers, to marginalized communities, to democratic institutions — and toward speculative scenarios that may never materialize.

Vallor: The AI Mirror and the Anthropomorphism Error

Shannon Vallor offers the most philosophically direct challenge to Part I's framing. In The AI Mirror (2024) she argues that today's AI systems are best understood not as emerging minds but as mirrors: trained on vast records of past human output, they reflect it back at us, magnified, distorted, and stripped of the lived experience that produced it. A reflection can look and behave remarkably like the thing it reflects without being that thing at all.

Two connected criticisms follow. The first is the anthropomorphism error. Talk of a superintelligence that "wants" outcomes, "resists" shutdown, or "deceives" its operators imports a vocabulary of intention and inner life that current systems do not have and that we have no reason to expect the next generation to acquire. Her objection is not that machines will never be dangerous; it is that describing the danger in mentalistic terms misdescribes where it comes from, and so points us at the wrong remedies. The second is the abdication of moral agency. The realistic catastrophe is not that machines take control but that we hand it over — letting systems incapable of moral judgment decide about hiring, sentencing, benefits, and targeting, then treating the result as though no one decided anything. The mirror is dangerous because we mistake it for an authority.

Vallor also thinks the doom narrative pays off rhetorically for those who tell it: framing AI as civilization-ending casts the people building it as the only ones qualified to save us from it, licensing urgency while deferring accountability for harms already occurring. But this is a claim about incentives, not a refutation. Her substantive argument is the one about mirrors and misdescription, and it would stand even if every AI researcher were a saint.

Key Thinker

Shannon Vallor (contemporary)

Shannon Vallor is an American philosopher of technology who holds the Baillie Gifford Chair in the Ethics of Data and Artificial Intelligence at the University of Edinburgh. She is best known for Technology and the Virtues, which applies virtue ethics to the design and use of digital technologies. She previously worked as an AI ethicist at Google Cloud and advises UK and EU regulators.

Vallor argues that both the existential-risk camp and its critics have paid too little attention to the moral character of the people and organizations building AI. A virtuous AI community would not just worry about technical alignment; it would cultivate epistemic humility, attention to context, solidarity with those harmed, and willingness to be accountable. The AI Mirror (2024) develops the argument that the real existential threat is not that AI will acquire a will of its own but that human beings will surrender the capacities — judgment, imagination, moral attention — that make us worth reflecting in the first place.

Narayanan and Kapoor: AI as Snake Oil

Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor, computer scientists at Princeton, offer a more empirical critique. Their 2024 book AI Snake Oil turns on a distinction the word "AI" hides. Generative AI — the systems that write text and produce images — genuinely works and is genuinely improving. Predictive AI — systems sold to forecast who will commit a crime, default on a loan, or need child-protective intervention — very often does not, frequently failing to outperform simple statistical baselines while being marketed as though it does. Lumping the two together lets vendors borrow the credibility of the first to sell the second.

Applied to existential risk, this yields the capabilities-gap objection. The x-risk argument needs a system that sets and pursues open-ended goals; current systems are narrow and brittle outside their training distribution, and several publicized "emergent capabilities" turned out to be artifacts of benchmark scoring. Extrapolating from here to a recursively self-improving agent is conjecture about a trajectory nobody can observe. In a 2025 essay, "AI as Normal Technology" , they press further: probability estimates for speculative catastrophe cannot carry the policy weight placed on them, and AI is better modeled on transformative-but-ordinary technologies like electricity, absorbed over decades and governed by the unglamorous instruments of regulation, liability, and institutional adaptation.

They are emphatically not arguing that AI is harmless. Bias, labor displacement, concentration of power, and degradation of the information ecosystem are serious and systemic — just not existential. The framing matters: the response to a near-term, measurable problem looks very different from the response to a speculative one, and a discourse organized around the second leaves the first unaddressed.

Bender, Gebru, and "Stochastic Parrots"

In a now-famous 2021 conference paper, Emily Bender, Timnit Gebru, Angelina McMillan-Major, and a fourth co-author writing as Shmargaret Shmitchell — widely reported to be Margaret Mitchell — asked whether language models had become "too big." Their central image is the stochastic parrot: a model stitches together linguistic forms observed in training according to probabilistic information about how they combine, without reference to meaning. Fluency is not comprehension, and a system can be confident and wrong in the same sentence.

Their catalogue of harms is more specific than it is often reported to be: the environmental and financial costs of ever-larger training runs; corpora too large for anyone to document, and therefore certain to encode their producers' biases; a field that mistakes scale for progress; and fluent, unmoored text taken as authoritative. The distinctively political claim — that extinction discourse actively displaces attention from these harms — came later, in a March 2023 joint statement by the paper's named authors responding to an open letter calling for a pause on large model training .

Gebru's departure from Google in December 2020, while the paper was under review, remains contested: Google called it a resignation, Gebru a firing after she was asked to retract the paper or remove her name. Either way it raised an unsettled question — who gets to publish critical research about systems their employer is building.

Torres and the TESCREAL Critique

Émile Torres offers perhaps the sharpest ideological critique. Torres coined the acronym TESCREAL — developed at length with Timnit Gebru in 2024 — for a loose but influential cluster combining Transhumanism, Extropianism, Singularitarianism, Cosmism, Rationalism, Effective Altruism, and Longtermism. These movements, they argue, share assumptions: that the most important ethical considerations concern the distant future; that expected-value reasoning is the right tool for all ethical decisions; that a small technical elite should make civilization-scale choices; and that present harms can be discounted against speculative future benefits.

On this view existential-risk discourse is not politically neutral. It serves wealthy technology entrepreneurs, elite research organizations, and the intellectuals they fund, by framing the important AI questions as ones requiring technical expertise (which they have) rather than democratic accountability (which would distribute power). The critique does not require believing anyone is a conscious bad actor; ideological frameworks can serve institutional interests even when adopted sincerely.

It is dangerous to distract ourselves with a fantasized AI-enabled utopia or apocalypse which promises either a "flourishing" or "potentially catastrophic" future.

Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major and Mitchell, statement on the "AI pause" letter, DAIR Institute (2023)

That sentence captures the core tension. The existential-risk framework is not self-evidently wrong; misaligned superintelligence may well be the most important AI problem. But adopting the framework carries opportunity costs. Every dollar spent on speculative alignment research is a dollar not spent on bias auditing, labor protections, or democratic AI governance; every policy discussion framed around hypothetical superintelligence is one not framed around the systems already making consequential decisions about people's lives. The critics are asking us to take that trade-off seriously.

Thought Questions

  1. Can the existential-risk case and the present-harms case be taken seriously at the same time? What would a policy agenda that did both look like?
  2. Vallor argues that talk of an AI that "wants" things misdescribes the danger. Does Bostrom's argument depend on that vocabulary, or can it be restated without any mentalistic language? Try restating it.
  3. Bender and Gebru call large language models "stochastic parrots." How does your own experience with AI tools bear on this? Have you seen evidence of genuine understanding, or very good pattern-matching?

Part III: Autonomous Weapons

The debate over superintelligence can feel abstract. The challenges posed by autonomous weapons are considerably more concrete. Militaries are already deploying AI-powered systems capable of identifying, tracking, and in some cases attacking targets with varying degrees of human oversight, raising hard questions about accountability, the laws of armed conflict, and the moral meaning of violence: if a machine kills a civilian, who — if anyone — is responsible?

Lethal Autonomous Weapons System (LAWS)

A weapons system that uses sensors and AI to identify, select, and engage targets without meaningful human control over the decision to use lethal force. Sometimes called "killer robots," though the term covers systems with widely varying levels of automation. The defining feature is that the lethal decision — not just the delivery mechanism — is delegated to the machine.

The Autonomy Spectrum

Weapons systems do not fit neatly into a binary of "human controlled" versus "fully autonomous." Paul Scharre, whose 2018 book Army of None remains the standard treatment, describes a spectrum of human involvement in lethal decisions. At one end a human pulls the trigger on every engagement; at the other a system detects, classifies, and attacks targets according to programmed criteria, with no human in any individual targeting decision.

The U.S. Phalanx close-in weapon system, which automatically engages incoming missiles to defend ships, is "human-on-the-loop": a human arms it and can shut it down, but individual engagements happen faster than human reaction time permits. South Korea's SGR-A1 sentry gun, reportedly tested along the demilitarized zone, is described as technically capable of engaging human targets autonomously, though its manufacturer says an operator must authorize any use of force. (Deployment details are not publicly confirmed, which is itself part of the governance problem.) These are operational systems raising real questions now.

🎖️

Key Thinker

Paul Scharre (contemporary)

Paul Scharre is a defense policy analyst and former U.S. Army Ranger who served in Iraq and Afghanistan, then worked in the Office of the Secretary of Defense on the Defense Department's first directive on autonomy in weapons systems. His 2018 book Army of None is therefore written from inside the policy machinery as well as from the field.

He lays out two kinds of argument against LAWS. The consequentialist argument holds that autonomous weapons will perform worse than humans in complex, ambiguous combat — more mistakes, more civilian deaths, escalation nobody intended. The deontological argument holds that delegating the decision to kill to a machine is intrinsically wrong regardless of reliability, because it strips the act of the moral weight killing demands.

Do not enlist Scharre as a straightforward abolitionist. He takes both arguments seriously and insists human judgment must be present in the use of lethal force, but he is openly skeptical that a comprehensive preemptive ban could be defined precisely enough or verified reliably enough to hold. His own proposals are narrower: a prohibition on anti-personnel autonomous weapons, and an agreed general principle that a human must always bear responsibility for decisions to take human life. For the case for an outright ban, look to Human Rights Watch and Stop Killer Robots rather than to Scharre.

The Laws of Armed Conflict

International humanitarian law requires combatants to distinguish military targets from civilians (distinction), to ensure expected civilian casualties are not disproportionate to anticipated military advantage (proportionality), and to take all feasible precautions to minimize civilian harm (precaution). Human Rights Watch argues that fully autonomous weapons cannot reliably apply these principles, which require contextual judgment of a kind current AI cannot perform.

The argument is not primarily about accuracy. A system could be highly accurate at identifying uniformed combatants and still fail the proportionality test, which requires weighing military value against expected civilian casualties in a way that is inherently contextual. Thresholds can be programmed, but they cannot anticipate every context — and the stakes of getting it wrong are irreversible.

Alongside this runs a second and more structural objection, developed by the philosopher Robert Sparrow and taken up by Human Rights Watch: even a perfectly reliable autonomous weapon would leave nobody to hold responsible when it kills someone it should not have.

What's the Argument?

The Responsibility Vacuum Problem

  1. When an autonomous weapon kills a civilian, responsibility must lie with some person or institution — designer, deploying commander, authorizing political authority, or some combination.
  2. Each can plausibly disclaim it: the designer could not anticipate every scenario; the commander deployed the system within its stated parameters; the political authority authorized a class of weapons, not individual shots.
  3. When responsibility is spread so diffusely that no one can be held accountable, accountability disappears — no punishment, no deterrence, no adequate incentive to prevent future harm.
∴ Fully autonomous weapons systems create a "responsibility vacuum" that is incompatible with both the rule of law and basic moral accountability for the use of lethal force.

This does not depend on believing autonomous weapons will be unreliable; it would apply to a perfectly accurate targeting system. The problem is structural. Autonomous weapons remove the human moral agent from the decision to kill, and no institutional arrangement can restore that agency after the fact. The result is violence without a perpetrator — precisely what the laws of armed conflict are designed to prevent.

Case Study

The Kargu-2 in Libya (2020): The First Autonomous Lethal Attack?

In March 2021 the United Nations Panel of Experts on Libya reported that, a year earlier, forces aligned with the Government of National Accord had used Kargu-2 loitering munitions — small drones made by the Turkish firm STM — against retreating fighters affiliated with Khalifa Haftar. The munitions, the Panel wrote, were "programmed to attack targets without requiring data connectivity between the operator and the munition: in effect, a true 'fire, forget and find' capability," and retreating forces were "hunted down and remotely engaged."

The passage is widely reported as the first autonomous killing in history, and the gap between what the report establishes and what it is said to establish is instructive. The Panel described a capability and a deployment. It did not state that anyone was killed by a munition operating without human supervision, and it did not investigate individual engagements; the manufacturer disputed the characterization; analysts have since disagreed about whether an operator retained meaningful oversight. The honest summary is that a threshold may have been crossed in Libya in 2020 and nobody can say for certain whether it was — which is exactly what makes autonomous weapons hard to regulate. A norm that cannot be verified cannot easily be enforced.

The case nonetheless became a focal point for advocates of a binding prohibition. The coalition Stop Killer Robots now comprises more than 250 organizations across some 70 countries. Diplomatic movement has been real but slow: the UN General Assembly adopted resolutions on autonomous weapons in December 2023 and December 2024, and the Secretary-General and the International Committee of the Red Cross urged states to conclude a legally binding instrument by 2026. As of mid-2026 no such treaty exists — which illustrates how hard agreement is when the militarily most significant states have strong incentives to keep developing the technology.

Thought Questions

  1. The deontological argument holds that delegating the decision to kill to a machine is intrinsically wrong even if the machine is highly accurate. Do you find it convincing?
  2. If autonomous weapons demonstrably reduced civilian casualties — because they feel no fear, revenge, or exhaustion — would that change your view of whether to permit them?
  3. How should "meaningful human control" be defined in international law? Is the ability to shut a system down enough, if no human authorizes each shot?

Part IV: AI and Biological Threats

Of the near-term AI risks attracting serious expert attention, the potential misuse of AI in biological research may be the most alarming. Unlike the alignment problem, which concerns hypothetical future systems, or autonomous weapons, which require military infrastructure, the biological threat involves technology that already exists and is widely accessible: AI systems trained on research literature and molecular databases can assist actors who would otherwise lack the expertise to design dangerous pathogens or chemical agents. That concern needs careful qualification — but it concerns tools in use today, not a system nobody has built.

Dual-Use Research of Concern (DURC)

Life sciences research that can reasonably be anticipated to yield knowledge, products, or technologies directly misapplicable to threaten public health, safety, or national security. The term was formalized in U.S. biosecurity policy after the 2001 anthrax attacks and debates over H5N1 influenza research, and is now central to discussions of AI and biological risk.

AI as a Capability Multiplier in Biology

The biosecurity researcher Jonas Sandbrink, in a widely cited 2023 preprint, argues that AI represents a qualitative change in the dual-use risk landscape — but insists the change is not uniform. Earlier bioweapons concerns focused on state actors with significant resources and specialized expertise. AI changes the calculation in two ways: it can lower the expertise threshold for designing dangerous agents, offering guidance on synthesis routes, protein engineering, or delivery to someone otherwise untrained; and it accelerates research past oversight mechanisms designed for the timescales of traditional laboratory work.

His most useful contribution here is a distinction between two kinds of tool. General-purpose large language models pose mainly an information hazard: they make existing dangerous knowledge easier to find and assemble, but do not create capability that was not already somewhere in the literature. Dedicated biological design tools — systems that predict protein structure or generate novel sequences — are different, because they can in principle produce agents that have never existed. The two call for different governance: screening chatbot outputs does nothing about the second category, and restricting protein-design models does nothing about the first.

None of this means an available AI system will hand anyone a working bioweapon blueprint. Major providers have guardrails aimed at dangerous biological requests, though jailbreaks remain possible; and the hardest steps in building a biological weapon are not the informational ones. Acquiring, synthesizing, culturing, stabilizing, and delivering a pathogen remain difficult physical problems that no amount of text generation solves. The concern is directional rather than immediate: as systems become more capable, more specialized, and more widely deployed in laboratories, capability plus accessibility creates risks that existing biosecurity frameworks were not designed to manage.

Case Study

AI Designs VX Analogs in Hours (2022)

In March 2022, Fabio Urbina and colleagues at Collaborations Pharmaceuticals published a short paper in Nature Machine Intelligence describing an alarming proof-of-concept . They inverted the objective function of their AI drug-discovery system, MegaSyn: instead of searching for molecules with beneficial pharmacological properties, they asked it for maximum predicted toxicity. Within six hours it generated roughly 40,000 candidates, including numerous variants of the nerve agent VX, some predicted to be more toxic than VX itself.

They published as a warning, not a blueprint. Nothing was synthesized; the experiment was entirely computational. Their point was that the same infrastructure built for beneficial drug discovery could be repurposed with trivial modifications, and that the research community needed to confront this before it became acute. They also wrestled publicly with the ethics of publication, giving enough detail to establish the threat's credibility while withholding synthesis information.

The experiment became a reference point in biosecurity policy and fed into screening protocols for AI in biological research — including the U.S. framework for screening synthetic nucleic acid orders and the 2023 executive order on AI, which required developers of dual-use biological AI to adopt safety protocols. That scaffolding has proved unstable: the executive order was rescinded in January 2025 and the screening framework has been under revision since a further order in May 2025, so the current U.S. position should be checked rather than assumed. The underlying challenge does not change with administrations: the line between beneficial and harmful AI-assisted biology is drawn by the intent of the user, not the design of the system.

Governing dual-use AI in research is hard. Simply restricting access is impractical: the underlying models are often open-source or widely distributed, and restricting them would also restrict beneficial work in medicine and materials science. More promising approaches screen at the synthesis level (flagging dangerous molecules before they are physically made), impose access controls on specialized biological AI tools, and monitor AI-assisted research at institutions working with dangerous agents. None is complete, and AI biosecurity governance remains at an early stage.

Thought Questions

  1. The VX researchers published as a warning while withholding synthesis details. Was that right? What should researchers weigh when deciding whether to publish dual-use findings?
  2. The biosecurity framework of the 2000s was designed to stop state actors. How does AI change the threat landscape, and what new governance tools might be needed?
  3. Sandbrink argues that language models are mainly an information hazard while biological design tools create genuinely new capability. If you could regulate only one, which, and what would you be giving up?

Part V: Longtermism and the Precautionary Principle

The existential-risk debate is also a philosophical question about how to reason and act under deep uncertainty about the long-run future. Two frameworks dominate: longtermism, which holds that the welfare of future generations deserves substantial weight, and the precautionary principle, which holds that when an action risks severe or irreversible harm the burden of proof lies with those who wish to proceed. Both are invoked by AI risk researchers; both have been seriously criticized.

Longtermism

Longtermism, developed most prominently by William MacAskill in What We Owe the Future (2022), holds that future people matter morally as much as present people, and that because there could be vastly more of them, our obligations to them could outweigh our obligations to the present generation. Preventing civilizational catastrophe is then among the highest moral priorities possible, because it is the only way to preserve the possibility of those lives.

Longtermism

The ethical view that positively influencing the long-run future is among the most important moral priorities. In its strong form it gives very high weight to the welfare of potential future people — who could vastly outnumber present ones — making extinction risk the dominant consideration in many policy decisions.

In its favor: longtermism provides a principled basis for taking extinction risks seriously and for investing in areas — AI safety, pandemic preparedness, asteroid defense — neglected precisely because their benefits accrue to people who cannot vote, lobby, or exert market pressure. The intuition that future generations matter is widely shared; longtermism takes it seriously in its full quantitative implications.

Against: the argument depends on hypothetical future people whose existence is deeply uncertain, and expected-value calculations involving trillions of lives are sensitive to small changes in probability estimates that are themselves extremely uncertain. Decision theorists call the sharpest version of this worry Pascal's mugging: multiply a tiny probability by a sufficiently astronomical payoff and almost any demand looks rational, impossible to argue with because nothing present can ever outweigh it. Critics including Émile Torres argue that such reasoning can justify almost any present sacrifice, and that in practice it has been used to discount urgent harms to real people in favor of speculative long-run flourishing.

The table simplifies — there are longtermists who take present harms seriously and present-focused critics who grant that long-run risks deserve attention — but it maps the default emphases of each orientation. The sharpest disagreement is not about values but epistemology: can we predict the long-run future reliably enough to justify weighting it heavily today?

The Precautionary Principle

The precautionary principle — roughly, "when in doubt, don't" — is often invoked in debates about AI risk. If we are genuinely uncertain whether powerful AI could cause catastrophic and irreversible harm, shouldn't we err on the side of caution? Cass Sunstein, who has studied the principle extensively, argues that it is both more appealing and more problematic than it first appears.

The appeal is that reversible caution seems wiser than irreversible action when the stakes are high. The problem, Sunstein argues, is that the principle is self-defeating when applied consistently, because almost any action risks serious harm to someone. Slowing AI carries risks too: diseases AI could have helped cure will kill more people, energy transitions it could have accelerated will be slower, economic and educational benefits will be delayed. Apply the principle to developing AI and you must also apply it to slowing AI — and the framework offers no principled way to choose.

Supporting argument

The asymmetry between catastrophic and recoverable risks is a principled reason for extra caution about AI. If we are wrong to go slowly, we lose near-term benefits that can be recovered later. If we are wrong to go quickly and catastrophe results, there is no recovery. Asymmetric risks warrant asymmetric caution — a version of the principle requiring not certainty about harm but only recognition that the downside is qualitatively different from the upside.

Objection

The asymmetry argument applies with equal force in the other direction. The benefits forgone by slowing AI — in medicine, climate, education, poverty reduction — are also potentially enormous, and they are more certain, more near-term, and more equitably distributed than the speculative gains from risk prevention. A principle that systematically discounts certain near-term benefits for uncertain long-term risk avoidance may do more harm than good.

Neither the precautionary principle nor expected-value reasoning gives a clean answer to how much caution is appropriate. Used together they can structure the debate: identifying the considerations on each side, exposing the empirical uncertainties that must be reduced, and guarding against both complacency and the paralysis that uses risk as a reason to avoid all difficult decisions.

The Other Tail: What If It Goes Right?

Almost everything in this chapter has concerned the bad tail. That is a strange way to argue about caution, because caution is always caution for the sake of something. Bostrom's Deep Utopia (2024) is the mirror image of Superintelligence, and worth reading precisely because the same author wrote both. Suppose alignment is solved, no war follows, and the technology delivers: machines can do everything humans currently do for a living, and do it better. Bostrom calls this a "post-instrumental" condition, in which human effort is no longer necessary for anything.

His claim is that this is not obviously a happy ending, and that we have thought about it far less carefully than about extinction. If work is optional, what is achievement? If a machine could have written your essay or comforted your friend better than you did, what did you contribute? He is not predicting misery; he is pointing out that the sources of meaning most cultures have relied on — necessity, scarcity, difficulty, being needed — would have to be replaced by something, and nobody has a good account of what. Readers of Chapter 8 will recognize the question about meaningful work, asked at civilizational scale and without the option of finding a different job.

This is not a change of subject. Both the precautionary principle and the expected-value argument assume we know what we are protecting. If the best plausible outcome is one we do not know how to evaluate, then some of the confidence on both sides of the doomsday debate is misplaced — and asking what a good future with AI would consist of is a precondition for having the risk debate well, not a soft postscript to it.

Thought Questions

  1. MacAskill argues that future people matter as much as present people. Do you agree? Does your answer change with how uncertain their existence is?
  2. What would it mean for AI governance to take existential risk and present harm seriously at once? Is the "both/and" framing coherent, or does it paper over real conflicts?
  3. Deep Utopia asks what would give life meaning if nothing we did was necessary. Name something you would still want to do well even if a machine could do it better, and say why.

Synthesis and Conclusion

It would be convenient if one side of this debate were clearly right and the other clearly wrong. Intellectual honesty requires sitting with the tensions.

What the Existential Risk Camp Gets Right

Systems significantly more capable than humans in all cognitive domains would be a world-historic event with consequences genuinely hard to predict or control, and the alignment problem is real: there is no proven method for ensuring a highly capable system reliably pursues beneficial goals rather than goals that merely resemble beneficial ones under training conditions. Dismissing these problems because they are uncomfortable, or because they are associated with wealthy Silicon Valley institutions, is not a serious response.

The camp is also right that some risks warrant preventive action under deep uncertainty, because the asymmetry between a prevented catastrophe and a forgone benefit can be enormous. We do not need certainty of extinction to justify investing in alignment research, capability monitoring, and international governance. A 1-in-100 chance of civilizational catastrophe is, by any reasonable accounting, urgent.

What the Critics Get Right

The existential-risk framing, as institutionalized, has demonstrably shifted attention and resources away from present harms. Systems deciding who gets a loan, whose benefits are cut, who is flagged by predictive policing, and whose job is automated cause real, measurable, unequally distributed harm today, and that does not become less urgent because a speculative future harm would be larger. The scale argument is only as strong as the probability estimates underlying it, and those estimates are deeply uncertain.

A discourse in which the primary risk is defined by wealthy researchers at elite institutions, concerns hypothetical systems, and calls for technical solutions from those same researchers deserves scrutiny even when its conclusions turn out to be right. Epistemic humility cuts both ways: it is a reason to take speculative risks seriously, and a reason to distrust frameworks that elevate the importance of those who construct them.

A Virtue-Ethics Synthesis

Vallor's virtue-ethics approach offers a way through. Instead of asking "which side is right?" — a question that assumes a possibly false binary — ask what habits of character should guide the people and organizations building, deploying, and governing AI. Epistemic humility means acknowledging that near-term and long-term risks are both real and uncertain, rather than resolving that uncertainty by prioritizing whatever is professionally interesting. Moral attention means keeping actual people, including the most vulnerable, central rather than abstracting them into expected-value calculations. Accountability means building regulatory bodies, auditing standards, and professional norms that hold developers responsible for the harms they cause now and the risks they create later.

The resulting "both/and" is not fence-sitting. The dichotomy between existential risk and present harm is partly a product of institutional competition for resources, and an ethical approach to AI needs the flexibility to treat alignment research and algorithmic accountability, autonomous weapons bans and bias auditing, biosecurity governance and labor protections as complementary rather than competing. How to allocate resources across them is genuinely difficult — but it cannot be answered well by a discourse that treats any one as obviously dominant.

Mirrors do not merely reveal things as they are: mirrors also magnify, occlude, and distort what is captured in their frame.

Vallor is writing about AI systems, but the sentence fits the debate about them. The existential-risk frame magnifies irreversibility and occludes distribution: very good at noticing that some mistakes cannot be undone, very bad at noticing who is already paying. The present-harms frame magnifies distribution and occludes irreversibility: very good at asking who bears the cost, rather too quick to assume anything speculative is unserious. Neither frame is neutral, and neither is dispensable. The question to carry out of this chapter is not "which side is right?" but "what is this framing helping me see, and what is it keeping out of the picture?"

How do you divide it, and which assumptions about probability, severity, and tractability are doing the work?
  2. Pick the position you personally find less plausible and write the strongest one-paragraph case for it that you can.

Summary

This chapter examined AI risk from several angles: the case for treating superintelligent AI as an existential threat, the critics who say that framing distracts from present harms, autonomous weapons and AI-enabled biological risk, longtermism and the precautionary principle, and the question of what a good outcome would even look like.

Key Points

  • Existential risk means threats that could permanently destroy humanity's potential or cause extinction; Ord's estimates — roughly 1 in 6 overall and 1 in 10 from AI, published in 2020 and revisited in 2024 without substantial change — make unaligned AI the largest single contributor.
  • The orthogonality and instrumental convergence theses carry Bostrom's argument, and both are more modest, and harder to dismiss, than the paperclip maximizer used to illustrate them.
  • The alignment problem is ensuring powerful systems pursue genuinely beneficial goals rather than ones that merely look beneficial under training conditions; Russell treats misspecified objectives as structural rather than a fixable bug.
  • The intelligence explosion argument (Bostrom, Yudkowsky) holds that a self-improving system could bootstrap beyond human control before anyone intervened — a step resting on empirical assumptions that remain unverified.
  • Critics make distinct arguments: Vallor's is that mentalistic talk of machines that "want" things misdescribes the danger, which is human abdication of moral judgment; Narayanan and Kapoor's is a capabilities-gap objection; Bender, Gebru, and Torres press the political claim that extinction discourse displaces attention from measurable present harms.
  • Lethal autonomous weapons raise Sparrow's "responsibility vacuum": when a machine kills without meaningful human control, no one can readily be held accountable.
  • Scharre is not an abolitionist: he insists humans must bear responsibility for lethal force, but doubts a comprehensive preemptive ban could be defined or verified, and proposes a narrower anti-personnel prohibition instead.
  • The Kargu-2 case (Libya, 2020) is often called the first autonomous killing, but the UN report established a capability and a deployment, not a confirmed autonomous fatality — and norms that cannot be verified cannot easily be enforced.
  • AI and biological risk are a present dual-use problem, as the 2022 VX analog experiment showed; Sandbrink's distinction matters — language models are chiefly an information hazard, while biological design tools can generate agents that never existed — and the hardest weaponization steps remain physical.
  • Longtermism (MacAskill) makes preventing existential risk dominant, while Sunstein argues the precautionary principle is self-defeating because it applies equally to the costs of slowing AI; Vallor's virtues — epistemic humility, moral attention, accountability — allow both long-run risk and present harm to be taken seriously without either dominating.
