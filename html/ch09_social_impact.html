Central Questions

  1. How can AI worsen environmental harms while being advertised as "smart" or "clean"?
  2. When does AI improve medicine and research, and when does it amplify bias, opacity, or unequal access?
  3. Can AI strengthen democratic governance, or does it mainly expand surveillance and bureaucratic control?
  4. Will AI reduce poverty, or deepen asymmetries of wealth, language, energy, and compute?
  5. What ethical principles should guide AI when the stakes are collective rather than individual?

Part I: AI Is Not "Virtual"

Most discussions of AI treat it as pure information — weightless, placeless, detached from the physical world. We speak of "the cloud" as if computing happened somewhere clean and frictionless, beyond geography and consequence. That framing is not just imprecise; it is ethically dangerous. It invites us to evaluate AI purely on what it produces while ignoring everything required to make that output possible.

In reality, artificial intelligence is a material and social system. It requires energy-intensive computation and cooling in massive data centers, water to keep them from overheating, and land and grid capacity competed for with other uses. It is built from minerals — cobalt, lithium, copper, rare earths — extracted through supply chains whose costs fall on communities far from where the AI is used. It relies on human labor at every stage: miners, data labelers, content moderators, technicians. And hardware replaced on short upgrade cycles becomes electronic waste, a burden that also falls unevenly.

A few concrete numbers make this vivid — though each has to be handled carefully, because environmental statistics about AI circulate much faster than they are checked. Training GPT-3, OpenAI's 2020 language model, is estimated to have consumed about 1,287 megawatt-hours of electricity — roughly the annual use of 120 American homes — and emitted around 550 tonnes of CO₂. The estimate comes from outside researchers , not OpenAI, and covers a single run of a model several generations old — it says nothing about the far larger runs since.

Microsoft reported consuming roughly 6.4 million cubic meters of water company-wide in 2022, a 34% increase over the previous year, most of it cooling its cloud data centers; in July 2022 alone its cluster outside West Des Moines, Iowa drew about 11.5 million gallons — around 6% of that water district's total for the month. And the Democratic Republic of Congo, one of the world's poorest countries, supplies roughly three-quarters of the world's mined cobalt — the metal in the lithium-ion batteries behind both our devices and data centers' backup power — often from mines with severe safety and environmental problems. Chatbot users are connected to these realities by supply chains designed not to be visible.

AI as a Material System

The view, developed most fully by Kate Crawford in The Atlas of AI, that AI is best understood not as software but as a planetary-scale system of extraction, classification, and control — with physical infrastructure, supply chains, labor relations, and environmental costs at every stage.

Why Collective Harms Are Hard to See

AI's social and environmental impacts are distributed, delayed, and offloaded. A single chatbot query feels trivial to the person who types it; multiplied across billions of daily interactions, it becomes a significant infrastructure load. The benefits are immediate and visible — a faster answer, a completed task. The costs are indirect: a marginal increase in electricity demand, a fractional draw on cooling water, a small contribution to hardware wear.

This structure — immediate, visible benefits; delayed, dispersed, often invisible costs — recurs across environmental and social harms, and it resists individual decision-making, because no individual's choice looks significant in isolation. Hence the need for institutional ethics — collective standards enforced through regulation, procurement policy, reporting requirements, and democratic oversight. Consumers choosing "greener" AI products is not a substitute for systemic accountability.

What's the Argument?

The Externalized Cost Argument

  1. An AI system's benefits — speed, convenience, capability, profit — accrue mainly to its users and the firms that build it.
  2. Many of that system's costs — mining hazards, water and grid strain near data centers, electronic waste — fall on communities that are neither users nor shareholders.
  3. Parties who decide whether to build a system, but do not bear its costs, have no reliable incentive to weigh those costs accurately.
  4. A decision procedure that systematically underweights real costs to identifiable people is unjust, however efficient it is.
∴ The uneven distribution of AI's benefits and burdens is an ethical problem in its own right, and one that better consumer purchasing choices cannot fix.

In plain English: AI's costs are not merely large but misdirected. Economists call this an externality — a cost of production borne by someone outside the transaction. If a data center's neighbors absorb the noise, the water draw, and the higher electricity prices, they pay part of the price of a product they may never use. The third premise does the real work, and it is a claim about incentives, not bad faith: even a scrupulous company has no market signal telling it how much harm it causes in a Congolese mining town, because that harm never appears on its balance sheet. Externalities are, by definition, problems individual choices cannot solve, because the chooser is not the payer.

Key Thinker

Kate Crawford (1974–present)

Kate Crawford is a senior principal researcher at Microsoft Research and a Research Professor at USC Annenberg. Her 2021 book The Atlas of AI: Power, Politics, and the Planetary Costs of Artificial Intelligence is the most comprehensive critical account of AI as a material system. She visited the sites that make AI possible — lithium mines in Nevada, data centers in the American West, Amazon warehouses, recycling facilities in Asia — to document what the marketing language of "the cloud" obscures.

Crawford argues that AI is not neutral but a system that extends existing structures of power and extraction. The firms promising AI will solve efficiency and climate problems are driving extraordinary demand for energy, minerals, and labor; the systems promising objectivity encode the hierarchies of their training data. AI is not simply bad, on her account — it just cannot be understood or governed without understanding what it is materially and politically. Her work belongs to a broader turn in AI criticism away from speculative futures toward the present and mundane.

Thought Questions

  1. Crawford insists on visiting mines, data centers, and recycling yards in person. What can you learn by standing next to a technology's infrastructure that reading cannot teach you? What might that method miss?
  2. The Externalized Cost Argument claims consumer choice cannot solve the problem. Is there any version of individual action — boycott, disclosure demands, voting with your usage — that would change the outcome?
  3. The chapter warns that AI statistics "circulate much faster than they are checked." Think of an environmental figure you have seen shared online. Could you say where it came from, what it measured, and what year it described?

Part II: Environment and Climate Change

AI's relationship to the environment is genuinely two-sided, and that complexity is itself ethically important. AI systems contribute to environmental problems through their energy and material demands; they may also help address them through better prediction and resource management. Understanding both sides is more useful than either dismissing AI's environmental costs or accepting "AI for climate" uncritically.

AI's Environmental Footprint

AI's environmental impact has several dimensions that no single metric like carbon emissions captures, and each raises distinct ethical and policy questions.

Table 9.1 — Dimensions of AI's Environmental Footprint
Dimension Why it matters
Electricity Training and inference require large and growing loads; the energy source matters as much as the quantity
Water Cooling can strain local water supplies, especially in drought-prone regions
Materials Chips depend on extraction, fabrication, and transport chains with their own carbon and labor costs
Land & grids Hyperscale data centers compete for space and grid capacity with homes, hospitals, and other uses
E-waste Short upgrade cycles create disposal burdens that fall unevenly on poorer communities

The International Energy Agency (IEA) is the standard reference here. Its April 2025 report Energy and AI put global data-center electricity use at roughly 415 TWh in 2024 — about 1.5% of world consumption. Ten months later, Electricity 2026 raised the 2025 figure to about 485 TWh: data-center demand grew roughly 17% in a year, against about 3% growth in global electricity demand overall. The IEA projects consumption will roughly double to about 950 TWh by 2030, around 3% of global electricity, with the AI-specific share growing fastest — up roughly 50% in 2025 alone, projected to triple by 2030. In the United States, data centers account for close to half of projected growth in electricity demand through 2030. For emissions, the IEA earlier attributed around 330 Mt CO₂e in 2020 to data centers and transmission networks together — about 0.9% of energy-related greenhouse-gas emissions, a baseline predating most of the build-out.

Two features teach something about reading a forecast. First, between the two IEA reports the headline 2030 projection barely moved — from roughly 945 TWh to roughly 950 TWh — while the near-term baseline rose substantially. The destination stayed put; the starting point caught up to it. A reader who saw only the 2026 headline might think the outlook had worsened dramatically, when the agency's view of 2030 was almost unchanged. Second, the 2026 report emphasizes bottlenecks: more than 2,500 GW of projects sit stalled in grid connection queues worldwide, and the IEA treats supply-chain and grid constraints as a real brake on the more aggressive scenarios. A projection is not a prediction. It says what would happen if a trend met no obstacles, and here the obstacles are visible.

These numbers are neither trivial nor catastrophic in isolation. What makes them politically significant is context: they compound pressures on already-stressed energy and water systems that are simultaneously being asked to support a renewable transition.

Case Study

Data Centers and Water in West Des Moines, Iowa (2022–2026)

Microsoft operates a cluster of data centers in West Des Moines, Iowa. In July 2022 — the month before OpenAI says it finished training GPT-4 on Microsoft hardware — the cluster drew about 11.5 million gallons of water, roughly 6% of everything the West Des Moines Water Works supplied that month, per the utility's own records, obtained and reported by the Associated Press in 2023. Evaporative cooling towers are cheap and effective, but they consume the water they use: it leaves as vapor.

The city responded not by refusing data centers but by setting a condition. In 2023 it told Microsoft it would approve more facilities only if peak water demand fell substantially. In 2024 Microsoft announced a closed-loop design that circulates the same water between servers and chillers and consumes almost none of it; it began piloting in 2026, and the next West Des Moines buildings are slated to use it. Local regulatory pressure, not corporate conscience alone, produced the change.

The case also carries a warning about evidence. In June 2025, and again in June 2026, central Iowa's water utility imposed mandatory lawn-watering bans, and much online commentary blamed the data centers. It was not the data centers: the bans were triggered by near-record nitrate concentrations in the Raccoon and Des Moines rivers, driven by farm runoff. Both things are true at once — the water draw was real and large enough to change municipal policy, and the most viral local water story of those years had a different cause. Getting this right means holding both facts, not choosing the more satisfying one.

The Rebound Problem

A common response to concerns about AI's energy footprint is that models keep getting more efficient — each query costs less energy than a year ago. True, but it does not follow that total energy use is declining. The rebound effect, also called the Jevons paradox, is a well-documented pattern in which efficiency improvements raise total resource use because cheaper systems get deployed more widely.

The Rebound Effect (Jevons Paradox)

The tendency for efficiency improvements to increase rather than decrease total resource use, because lower unit costs make a technology more accessible and encourage wider deployment. Applied to AI: cheaper inference means more queries, more applications, and more total energy demand, even as cost per query falls.

Applied to AI: as models get cheaper per query, firms integrate them into more products at larger scales, inference volume rises dramatically, and aggregate electricity and hardware demand still grows even as unit costs fall. This is why "smaller and cheaper models" cannot be straightforwardly celebrated as an environmental solution. History offers analogies. Fuel-efficient cars after the 1970s oil crisis did not cut gasoline consumption proportionately — Americans drove more miles because driving was cheaper. LED lighting increased total light consumption, because lights went into more places and stayed on longer. Efficient jet engines were followed by more flights and greater fuel burn. Each time the efficiency gain was real; the savings were offset by expanded use.

What's the Argument?

The Rebound Argument

  1. Making a technology more efficient lowers the resource cost of each unit of use.
  2. When unit cost falls, the technology is built into more products and used far more often.
  3. If total use expands faster than efficiency improves, total resource consumption rises even though each use has become cheaper.
  4. The cost of an AI query has fallen dramatically, and the volume of AI queries has risen faster still.
∴ Efficiency improvements in AI cannot by themselves be counted as environmental progress; the measure that matters is total resource use, not cost per query.

The argument is easy to overstate. It does not say efficiency is bad, only that efficiency and reduction are different things and the first does not automatically deliver the second. Premise 3 contains a real empirical condition — "if total use expands faster than efficiency improves" — and it can fail. Refrigerator standards did cut household refrigeration energy absolutely, because there is a ceiling on how many refrigerators a household wants. The worry about AI is that no such ceiling is in view once each task costs a fraction of a cent. The upshot is a test for any sustainability claim: if emissions per query fell 40%, ask what happened to the number of queries.

Training and inference also differ. Early public discussion focused on the spectacular energy cost of training frontier models, but evidence suggests inference — running the model to answer actual queries — is the larger long-run burden, because it scales with adoption. AI's environmental story is not about a few dramatic training events; it is about what happens when AI becomes ordinary infrastructure embedded in search, office software, medicine, and government services.

AI for Climate: A Comparative Judgment

AI is also deployed for climate applications, and this is not merely marketing. There are genuine use cases where it can help reduce emissions or adapt to a changing climate.

The case for AI as a climate tool

AI can improve grid balancing and demand forecasting, easing the integration of intermittent renewables. It can optimize building efficiency, cutting heating and cooling waste at scale, and accelerate materials discovery for clean technologies. Satellite monitoring can detect wildfires and floods faster, and crop-risk forecasting can help farmers adapt. These are real uses creating genuine public value — sometimes more value than the energy they consume.

Objection: Much "AI for climate" is marketing

The same firms promising AI-powered sustainability are among the largest drivers of data-center energy demand. Are the climate benefits real, or mostly pilots and demos? Are firms comparing AI's footprint to realistic alternatives, or to an implausibly inefficient baseline? Are they disclosing full costs — hardware, water, grid externalities? This does not prove AI cannot help with climate; it shows why environmental claims require evidence and independent auditing, not branding.

What's the Argument?

The Comparative Justification Argument

  1. Every deployed AI system imposes real material costs: electricity, water, minerals, land, and eventually waste.
  2. Some AI systems avert harms — earlier wildfire detection, better grid balancing, faster flood warning — plausibly greater than the harms their operation causes.
  3. Others avert no comparable harm; they deliver convenience, novelty, or marginal commercial advantage.
  4. A cost is justified only when it purchases a benefit at least as weighty as itself.
∴ Whether AI is environmentally justified is not one question about AI in general but a separate question about each application, answerable only with evidence about that application.

This is why the chapter refuses both available slogans. "AI is an environmental disaster" and "AI will save the climate" are claims about a category containing wildfire detection systems, protein-folding models, warehouse routing software, and advertising-copy generators — things with nothing in common but the technique used to build them. Premise 4 is a familiar principle of practical reasoning, one you would apply without hesitation to a medical procedure's risks, and it forces the comparison to be made case by case.

Two cautions follow. The standard is demanding: someone has to measure both the costs and the averted harms, and firms disclose far more about the second than the first. And it is silent about distribution — a system could pass the comparative test in aggregate while the harms fall on one population and the benefits on another, exactly what the Externalized Cost Argument warned about. Comparative justification is necessary, not sufficient.

Thought Questions

  1. What standard should judge whether an energy-intensive AI application is environmentally justified? Is net emissions reduction sufficient, or does the distribution of benefits matter too?
  2. If efficiency gains can increase rather than decrease total energy use, how should that change the way we read companies' environmental claims?
  3. In West Des Moines the water use was real, and the widely shared story blaming data centers for the watering ban was wrong. Does getting a detail like that wrong damage the underlying case against data-center water use, or is it beside the point?

Part III: Medicine and Research

Medicine is often presented as AI's strongest case — the domain where the benefits are most tangible and hardest to dismiss as convenience or commercial gain. A model that helps a radiologist catch a tumor she would have missed saves lives in a direct and comprehensible way. That makes medical AI worth taking seriously on its merits, and worth examining critically, because the stakes of getting it wrong are correspondingly high.

Scale and Concentration of Medical AI

AI deployment in medicine has grown dramatically. Stanford's AI Index reports that the U.S. Food and Drug Administration authorized 223 AI-enabled medical devices in 2023 alone, up from just six in 2015. The agency's public list passed 1,500 entries in early 2026 and continues to rise. It is revised several times a year, which is why this chapter gives no exact figure: any precise count is stale before the semester ends.

Two features of that list matter more than its length. The first is concentration. Roughly three-quarters of the authorized devices are radiology devices, because medical images are abundant, standardized, and well suited to the classification and anomaly-detection tasks current systems excel at. Cardiovascular and neurology follow at a distance; most of medicine barely appears. Adoption is real, and narrow. A student who reads "1,500 AI devices approved" and pictures AI spread evenly across medicine has misread it.

The second feature is more instructive: the list is not what it appears to be. The FDA itself cautions that it is "not a comprehensive resource" — it is assembled by keyword-searching the public summaries accompanying device authorizations, so a device whose paperwork used different vocabulary may simply be absent, and inclusion depends on what a manufacturer wrote rather than on what the software does. What looks like a census is really a search result. That does not make it useless — it is the best public inventory available, and its broad trends are almost certainly right — but the number carries an error bar it never displays. Asking how a statistic was constructed, not merely how large it is, is among this chapter's most transferable skills; we will need it again in Part V.

A Scientific Success: AlphaFold

The clearest example of AI delivering transformative scientific value is AlphaFold, DeepMind's protein-structure prediction system. Predicting how a protein folds from its amino-acid sequence — the "protein folding problem" — was among the hardest unsolved problems in structural biology, because a protein's three-dimensional structure determines its function and the search space is astronomically large.

AlphaFold broke through at the 2020 CASP competition, and the public database built from it now holds roughly 200 million predicted structures, freely available. In 2024 the work received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry: half to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for structure prediction, half to David Baker for computational protein design. It is a proof of concept — AI can genuinely accelerate research in ways that create broad social value, especially when paired with open access and public goals.

Scientific promise, however, does not erase concerns about access, intellectual property, and data control. When AI accelerates drug discovery, who can afford the resulting drugs — and who owns the patents — remains as urgent as ever. Faster discovery is not better distribution.

Risks of Medical AI

Medical AI's risks are not limited to inaccuracy. The most serious arise when systems perform well on average but unevenly across populations, or when their place in clinical workflows changes how clinicians think and decide.

Table 9.2 — Key Risks of Medical AI
Risk Why it matters
Bias in training data Underrepresented groups — women, people of color, patients in lower-income settings — get worse predictions because they appear less often in training data
Opacity Clinicians and patients may not know why a recommendation was made, complicating consent and professional judgment
Automation bias and overreliance When clinicians defer without independent scrutiny, the human check disappears and errors get harder to catch
Privacy and consent Medical datasets are intimate and hard to anonymize; training at scale raises questions about patients' rights over their data
Access inequality Advanced diagnostics reach well-resourced institutions first, widening rather than closing health gaps

The World Health Organization has argued that AI in health should be governed by human rights, public accountability, and the needs of health workers and patients — not only by technical performance metrics. The framing refuses to let "accuracy on a benchmark" suffice for deployment. Consider a system with 95% accuracy overall but 82% for patients with darker skin. Deploying it without acknowledging that disparity is an ethical failure, not merely a technical limitation.

Case Study

Hypothetical: Triage, Scarcity, and Algorithmic Allocation

Suppose a hospital facing resource constraints implements an AI system to prioritize appointments, flag likely no-shows, and rank patients by clinical risk. The case seems straightforward: limited staff and equipment are used more efficiently, and some patients receive earlier intervention.

But the critical questions concern how the model was trained. If the training data reflects a history of under-treating certain populations — because those patients avoided care due to cost, distrust, or lack of access — the model may read low utilization as lower need, ranking disadvantaged patients below their actual risk. Social disadvantage becomes encoded as "lower compliance," and the algorithm's apparent objectivity makes it harder to challenge.

Most importantly, the efficiency gains may depoliticize scarcity itself. If the algorithm is seen as allocating limited resources optimally, the underlying question — why are resources insufficient? — recedes from view. Allocating scarce care efficiently is not the same as making the health system just.

Thought Questions

  1. If a medical AI improves average accuracy but performs noticeably worse for one vulnerable group, should it be deployed? Do you weight overall utility, equal treatment, protection of the worst-off, or informed patient choice most heavily, and why?
  2. The FDA's device list is a keyword-derived sample the agency says is "not a comprehensive resource." Whose responsibility is it to communicate that caveat — the agency's, the journalist's, or the reader's?
  3. Radiology accounts for roughly three-quarters of authorized AI devices. Does that tell you where AI is useful in medicine, or only where the training data happens to be?

Part IV: Good Governance

Democratic governance has always faced a tension between efficiency and legitimacy. Efficient government decides quickly, cheaply, with uniform rule application. Legitimate government makes decisions citizens can understand, contest, and hold accountable — decisions that respect rights and remain correctable. AI intensifies the tension by promising efficiency at precisely the level — large-scale, consequential, population-affecting decisions — where legitimacy requirements are most demanding.

What Good AI Governance Requires

The OECD's AI Principles — first adopted in 2019, updated in May 2024, and now adhered to by 47 parties, including every OECD member state and the European Union — identify inclusive growth, human rights and democratic values, transparency, robustness, and accountability as core requirements for trustworthy AI. These are not technical standards but normative commitments about what relationship public institutions should have with the citizens they govern.

Table 9.3 — A Public-Sector AI Checklist
Question Why it matters
Can citizens appeal the decision? Without appeal, errors become entrenched rather than corrected
Can the system be explained? Public power should not hide behind black boxes; citizens are owed reasons
Is the model audited for bias? Unequal burdens fall on already-vulnerable groups, and detecting that takes auditing
Is data collection proportionate? Administrative convenience does not override privacy rights
Does meaningful human review remain? "Human in the loop" should not be a fiction covering automated decisions

The OECD notes that governments are cautious adopters of AI relative to the private sector — precisely because public decisions affect rights, not merely convenience, and accountability requirements are stricter. The caution is appropriate: the legitimacy logic of democratic governance imposes constraints that the efficiency logic of markets does not.

The Governance Triangle

Public-sector AI almost always claims to improve one of three goods: efficiency (faster, cheaper decisions at scale), fairness (equal treatment, non-discrimination), or accountability (explanation, appeal, assigned responsibility). The political question is what gets sacrificed when one corner is maximized without attention to the others.

What's the Argument?

The Efficiency–Legitimacy Tension in Democratic Governance

  1. Legitimacy requires that citizens be able to understand, contest, and hold accountable the decisions that affect them.
  2. AI in public administration is often opaque, non-contestable, and diffuse about responsibility — satisfying efficiency while undermining legitimacy.
  3. A government that decides more quickly and cheaply, but less explainably and appealably, has not necessarily become better.
∴ Efficiency gains from AI in government cannot substitute for the transparency, contestability, and accountability that democratic legitimacy requires.

Key Thinker

Virginia Eubanks (1972–present)

Virginia Eubanks is a political scientist at the University at Albany, SUNY, and the author of Automating Inequality: How High-Tech Tools Profile, Police, and Punish the Poor (2018). Through case studies in welfare, child protective services, and healthcare allocation, she documents a recurring pattern: digital tools introduced to manage scarce public resources tend to reproduce and intensify existing inequalities.

Eubanks identifies a "digital poorhouse" — automated systems that discipline and surveil poor people rather than helping them. When Indiana automated and privatized welfare eligibility under a 2006 contract with IBM and its partners, denials rose by more than half — over a million in three years — many recorded as "failure to cooperate" after a fax went astray; the state cancelled the contract in 2009. Los Angeles used a predictive model to prioritize housing for homeless people, producing opaque rankings that were hard to challenge. Allegheny County's child-welfare model drew disproportionately on social-services data — data poor families generate simply by being poor — to rank families for investigation.

Her central argument: automated systems in government make exclusion look neutral and technical when it reflects political choices about who matters and how public resources should be distributed. The algorithm does not solve the scarcity; it manages it while obscuring the political decisions involved.

The general pattern: governments introduce automated screening, ranking, or fraud detection when they face scarcity, political pressure, or public distrust, and the burdens fall most heavily on people already dependent on public institutions. Automation appears to solve a technical problem (efficient allocation) while the political problem (inadequate resources, structural disadvantage) remains intact. AI becomes a technology for making exclusion look objective.

The pattern is visible across countries. In the United Kingdom, the Home Office ran a "visa streaming" tool from 2015 that sorted applications into red, amber, and green risk tiers partly on the basis of nationality, with red-tier applications drawing far more scrutiny and far more refusals. In August 2020 the department suspended it rather than defend it in a judicial review brought by the Joint Council for the Welfare of Immigrants and the campaign group Foxglove, who argued it was discriminatory under the Equality Act 2010 and irrational at common law. In the United States, Allegheny County's child-welfare models drew so heavily on data generated by poverty itself — welfare applications, housing assistance, public mental-health visits — that families could be flagged partly for being poor. The algorithm did not create the inequality; it automated and laundered it.

Be precise about what automation is and is not responsible for. Britain's better-known Windrush scandal, in which long-settled British residents of Caribbean origin were wrongly classified as illegal immigrants and some detained or deported, was not an algorithmic failure. It grew out of the "hostile environment" policy, which shifted the burden of proving lawful status onto individuals, combined with the Home Office's own destruction of the landing-card records that would have supplied that proof. The lesson cuts both ways: automated systems can encode injustice, and ordinary bureaucratic policy can do the same without any software. Blaming the algorithm is sometimes accurate and sometimes a way of avoiding the political question.

Case Study

The Dutch Childcare Benefits Scandal (2005–2021)

Between roughly 2005 and 2019, the Dutch tax administration used a risk-classification system to find suspected fraud in childcare benefit applications. It was self-learning, largely undocumented, and — as later investigations established — used dual nationality as an input variable, in violation of Dutch and European non-discrimination law. Families it flagged were treated as fraudsters rather than people who might have made a paperwork error, and agency policy required full repayment of every benefit received.

About 26,000 parents were wrongly accused. Repayment demands typically ran from €20,000 to €60,000, due in full, often with penalties and no payment plan. Families lost homes and businesses; in more than 1,600 cases children were removed by youth-protection services. Appeals were slow and, for years, largely futile, because the model's outputs carried the authority of an objective finding while its reasoning was inaccessible even to the officials relying on it.

A parliamentary inquiry reported in December 2020 under the title Unprecedented Injustice , and in January 2021 Prime Minister Mark Rutte's third cabinet resigned over the affair. This is the sharpest illustration of the Efficiency–Legitimacy Tension. Nothing about the system was technically exotic; what made it catastrophic was institutional — no explanation, no workable appeal, a presumption of guilt, and a discriminatory variable nobody outside the agency could see. Compensation and reform were still running years later: automated harms are far cheaper to inflict than to undo.

Thought Questions

  1. Which matters more in public administration: deciding quickly, or deciding explainably and appealably? Can a government at scale do both well, or is a trade-off unavoidable?
  2. Eubanks argues that automated government systems tend to discipline and punish the poor rather than help them. Is that inherent to such systems, or a contingent failure better design and oversight could correct?
  3. In the Dutch case, officials could not explain the model's outputs either. Does that make them less blameworthy, or more — given that they acted on reasoning they did not understand?

Part V: Poverty and Global Inequality

The most consequential questions about AI's social impact may be global rather than national. The World Bank's 2024 Poverty, Prosperity and Planet report estimated that about 700 million people — 8.5% of the world — were living in extreme poverty on less than $2.15 per day, and about 3.5 billion, 44%, on less than $6.85. Sub-Saharan Africa held 67% of the world's extreme poor in 2024, with 16% of global population.

Those figures deserve exactly the scrutiny we gave the FDA's device list. In June 2025 the World Bank moved its international poverty line from $2.15 in 2017 prices to $3.00 in 2021 prices, folding in new household surveys from more than 160 countries. The same underlying world was suddenly recorded as holding roughly 125 million more extremely poor people; the Bank's estimate for 2025 is about 808 million, close to one person in ten . Nobody became poorer the day the line moved; the count changed because the definition did. Not a reason to dismiss poverty statistics — the revision made them more accurate — but a reason to ask of any headline figure who drew the line, in what currency, and for what year.

Against this background, claims that AI will transform human welfare need careful evaluation: not just whether particular applications provide value, but whether AI development as a whole reproduces or disrupts the structural inequalities that generate poverty.

The Case for AI in Development

Advocates for "AI for development" argue that the technology can genuinely reduce poverty by extending scarce expertise to underserved populations. A translation tool can help a non-English speaker navigate a benefits office. A mobile agricultural assistant giving localized weather alerts and crop advice may reach farmers who lack extension services. Low-cost diagnostics could identify disease earlier where physicians are scarce. These claims are not empty.

The Structural Critique

But the optimistic story depends on power relations that AI, in its current form, tends to reproduce rather than disrupt. The structural questions are stark: Who owns the models, the cloud infrastructure, and the chips? Whose languages appear in training data, and whose are absent or distorted? Which countries bear the environmental costs of extraction and disposal? Who captures the profits when AI is deployed in lower-income countries?

Key Thinker

Amartya Sen (1933–present)

Amartya Sen is an Indian economist and philosopher at Harvard University, awarded the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1998. His Development as Freedom (1999) challenged the view that development is mainly about increasing GDP. Development, he argued, is the expansion of substantive human freedoms — people's real capabilities to live lives they have reason to value. Poor health, illiteracy, lack of political voice, and social exclusion are not just consequences of poverty; they are forms of it.

Applied to AI, Sen's framework shifts the question from "Does this raise productivity?" to richer ones: Does it expand human capabilities and agency? Does it strengthen local institutional capacity, or create dependency on foreign platforms? Does it give people control over decisions affecting their lives? These are not abstract questions. A mobile AI health assistant that provides useful information while collecting health data for a foreign company, routing it through infrastructure the user cannot control, and generating profits entirely outside the user's country may improve one measurable outcome while diminishing others Sen's framework requires us to count.

Development is not just higher output. It is an expansion of what people are actually able to do and be.

A paraphrase of Amartya Sen's central claim in Development as Freedom (1999)

That sentence is a compression rather than a quotation, but it captures what makes Sen's framework useful. Output is a proxy; capability is what the proxy stands for. Separate the two and you can ask a question no productivity statistic answers — whether the people an AI system touches end up with more genuine options than before.

Algorithmic Bias and Racial Justice

Within wealthy countries, AI's impact on inequality has a racial dimension that needs its own analysis. Two scholars have been central to that critique.

🔬

Key Thinkers

Safiya Umoja Noble & Ruha Benjamin

Safiya Umoja Noble's Algorithms of Oppression (2018) documented how search engines systematically return demeaning results for searches related to Black women and other marginalized groups, reflecting and amplifying biases in their training data. Calling search algorithms "neutral" is false in a specific sense: they encode the prejudices in the documents they index and the commercial incentives of the firms that build them.

Ruha Benjamin's Race After Technology (2019) extended the analysis to facial recognition, criminal justice prediction, healthcare allocation, and hiring tools under the concept of the "New Jim Code": ostensibly neutral systems that encode racial hierarchy while presenting themselves as objective, fair, or even liberatory. Her insight is that the problem is not just that biased people build biased systems; it is that systems trained on data from unjust arrangements reproduce those arrangements, and technical objectivity makes this harder to see and to challenge.

Extractive AI and the Question of Dependency

The pattern across environmental, labor, and development concerns is sometimes called extractive AI or AI colonialism: data, labor, energy, and materials drawn from communities with less power, while benefits and control centralize in a few firms and wealthy countries.

Case Study

Hypothetical: AI for Development — or a New Dependency?

Consider an AI-powered agricultural advisory service in a low-income country. It provides localized crop advice and weather alerts by mobile phone, drawing on satellite data, climate records, and agronomic models. Farmers who use it report better decisions about planting dates and irrigation. This is a real benefit.

But the infrastructure is owned by a foreign cloud provider. The data farmers input — crops, yields, land — sits on foreign servers under terms most users do not read. The model was trained mainly on data from wealthier agricultural regions, potentially making its recommendations less accurate for local conditions and crops. The company's business model depends on growing user data and subscription revenue.

Has development occurred? Some outcomes improved. But local agricultural institutions are weaker relative to the foreign platform, farmers depend on infrastructure they cannot inspect or govern, and data representing their land and labor generates value elsewhere. Sen's framework asks us to count all of that, not just the planting-date improvement.

Thought Questions

  1. The World Bank's extreme-poverty count rose by roughly 125 million when the line was redrawn in 2025, without anyone's circumstances changing. Does that make you trust such figures more or less? What would you want to know before quoting one?
  2. Can you think of an AI application that raises a measurable output while reducing relevant human capabilities? Is the net effect positive by Sen's standard?
  3. Noble and Benjamin argue that "neutral" algorithms can reproduce racial hierarchy. What would a genuinely unbiased system look like — or does the data always carry historical injustice?

Key Points

This chapter has argued that AI must be evaluated not only by what it does but by how it redistributes benefits, risks, resources, and decision-making power. Its impacts on the environment, medicine, governance, and global inequality are not secondary concerns to address after the technology is built; they are constitutive of what it is for. Sustainability, human benefit, legitimacy, and justice are four tests that must be applied together.

Key Points

  • AI as a material system (Kate Crawford): AI is not weightless software but a planetary-scale infrastructure of extraction, energy, labor, and logistics, so its ethics must be traced through the whole supply chain, not just model outputs.
  • Judging environmental claims takes two moves: the rebound effect means efficiency gains do not automatically cut total resource use, and the comparative test means the real question is whether a specific application averts enough harm to justify its material costs.
  • Medical AI shows genuine promise — more than 1,500 FDA-authorized devices and a Nobel-recognized breakthrough in AlphaFold — but adoption is narrow (roughly three-quarters radiology) and the risks are serious.
  • Statistics are constructed: the FDA's device list is a keyword-derived sample the agency says is "not a comprehensive resource," and the World Bank's extreme-poverty count rose by roughly 125 million when the line was redrawn in 2025 — ask how a number was made, not only how big it is.
  • Virginia Eubanks showed that automated government systems often discipline and punish poor people rather than help them, making exclusion look objective while the political problem of scarcity goes unaddressed.
  • The efficiency–legitimacy tension: AI can make government faster while undermining the transparency, contestability, and accountability that legitimacy requires, and a faster government is not necessarily a better one.
  • Noble and Benjamin documented how "neutral" algorithmic systems can reproduce racial hierarchy while presenting themselves as objective — what Benjamin calls the "New Jim Code."
  • Sen's capabilities framework: development means expanding what people can actually do and be, so the test for an AI system is whether it expands agency and local capacity or deepens dependency.
  • Extractive AI / AI colonialism: data, labor, energy, and materials are drawn from communities with less power while benefits and control centralize in a few firms and wealthy countries.
  • A four-test framework: evaluate any consequential deployment against sustainability, human benefit, legitimacy, and justice — environmental cost, who gains, whether it can be contested, and how burdens fall.

Thought Questions

  1. Apply the four tests — sustainability, human benefit, legitimacy, justice — to one AI system you actually use. Which test does it pass most clearly? Which does it fail?
  2. We do not hold other technologies — bricks, antibiotics, spreadsheets — to the standard this chapter applies to AI. Is AI genuinely different, or is this a standard we should have been applying all along?
  3. Advising a state legislature drafting rules for AI in public agencies, which single requirement from this chapter would you insist on first, and what would it cost?
