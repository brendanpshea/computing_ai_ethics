Introduction: What Is Virtue Ethics?

Most ethical debates about technology focus on actions: Should platforms remove harmful content? Should companies collect data on children? These questions share a framing — ethics as the sorting of permitted actions from prohibited ones. A different tradition asks something more fundamental: not "What should I do?" but "What kind of person am I becoming?"

This is the central question of virtue ethics, the oldest sustained tradition in Western moral philosophy, rooted in Aristotle. Rather than a decision procedure for individual acts, it asks about character — the stable dispositions to perceive, feel, and act that define who we are over time. Technology is then a moral issue not because particular apps produce bad outcomes, but because the daily practices of digital life shape the people we are becoming.

Chapter 1 traced a recurring pattern: each new technology reshapes not just what people can do but how they think and relate. Writing atrophied oral memory; print enabled mass propaganda; mass media created the preconditions for totalitarianism. Virtue ethics names what is at stake: our intellectual and moral character. If character is formed by what we repeatedly do, the habits built by hours of daily screen time are not ethically neutral.

This chapter develops three arguments: that social media and AI may undermine intellectual virtues, erode moral virtues, and cumulatively threaten eudaimonia, the Aristotelian conception of a flourishing life. We then survey policy responses and the strongest objections.

We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act, but a habit.

Will Durant, The Story of Philosophy (1926), summarizing Aristotle

That sentence is almost always credited to Aristotle. It is not his. The words are Will Durant's , written in 1926 to summarize Aristotle on habituation in Book II of the Nicomachean Ethics ; over the following century the quotation marks quietly migrated from the summarizer to the philosopher summarized. A vivid paraphrase attached to a famous name travels further than an accurate citation, and every repetition makes the attribution look better established than it is. Durant captures Aristotle faithfully, which is why it spread — but the difference between "Aristotle wrote this" and "someone once summarized Aristotle this way" is exactly the distinction the intellectual virtues in this chapter protect.

Aristotle's Ethical Framework

Aristotle (384–322 BCE). Roman marble copy of a Greek bronze by Lysippos, c. 330 BCE. Museo Nazionale Romano, Rome. Public domain.

Aristotle (384–322 BCE)

Born in Stagira, Aristotle studied at Plato's Academy for twenty years before founding the Lyceum in Athens. He tutored the young Alexander the Great and was known to medieval scholars simply as "the Philosopher." His Nicomachean Ethics, named for his son Nicomachus, remains one of the most carefully argued works of moral philosophy ever written. Unlike Plato, who grounded ethics in ideal Forms, Aristotle began with the observable world: How do virtuous people actually live?

Aristotle begins the Nicomachean Ethics with a simple observation: every action aims at some good. We eat to satisfy hunger, study to acquire knowledge, work to earn money. But is there some highest good, pursued for its own sake rather than as a means? He argues that the answer is eudaimonia, usually translated as "happiness" or "flourishing," but richer than either word conveys.

Eudaimonia

Aristotle's term for human flourishing: the activity of the soul in accordance with virtue, sustained over a complete life. It names a way of living well, not a way of feeling good.

Eudaimonia is neither the pleasant emotional state of a good day nor the life satisfaction you report when surveyed. It is what your life amounts to when you have exercised your distinctively human capacities — above all, reason — in an excellent way. A person can feel pleasure while doing something vicious; a person living in eudaimonia is, by definition, living well and acting rightly.

The last row does the most work. Hedonic happiness and life satisfaction report how a life seems from the inside, and both can be mistaken; eudaimonia is a claim about how the life is actually going. Social media is very good at momentary pleasure. Whether those pleasures contribute to flourishing or undermine it is a separate question.

Virtue and the Doctrine of the Mean

How does one achieve eudaimonia? Through virtue (in Greek, aretē), a stable disposition to act, feel, and perceive in an excellent way. Virtues are not rules followed from outside but traits of character internalized through practice. The courageous person does not overcome fear by an act of will on each occasion — courage has become part of who they are.

Aristotle famously describes each virtue as a mean between two extremes: courage between cowardice and recklessness, generosity between miserliness and profligacy. The mean is not a mathematical midpoint but what suits the situation and the person — appropriate patience with a search engine differs from appropriate patience in a friendship. The person with practical wisdom, phronesis, is the one who can perceive what virtue requires in each case.

Aristotle distinguishes two categories of virtue. Intellectual virtues are excellences of the reasoning mind: theoretical wisdom (sophia), practical wisdom (phronesis), scientific understanding (episteme), intuitive reason (nous), and craft knowledge (techne). Moral virtues are excellences of character: courage, temperance, justice, generosity, honesty, friendliness. Phronesis is the "master virtue," required to apply all the others correctly; it cannot be reduced to rules or algorithms.

Phronesis (practical wisdom)

The capacity to perceive what a particular situation calls for and to act accordingly. Aristotle treats it as the master virtue, because every other virtue depends on knowing how much, when, toward whom, and in what manner it should be exercised.

Crucially, both types of virtue are acquired through practice. As Aristotle writes, "We become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts." The environment in which we practice therefore matters immensely — which is why technology is a moral concern at the deepest level. It structures the environment in which we practice being human.

  1. Aristotle says each virtue is a mean between excess and deficiency. What would the mean be for time spent online — and who or what should determine it, you or the platform?
  2. Can you describe a skill you possess where you can see the right move without being able to state the rule you are following? How close is that to what Aristotle means by phronesis?
  3. Suppose someone reports being very satisfied with a life you consider wasted. Does Aristotle's account give you any grounds to disagree with them, and should it?

The Modern Revival of Virtue Ethics

For much of the twentieth century, moral philosophy was dominated by Kantian deontology (ethics as rule-following) and utilitarian consequentialism (ethics as maximizing well-being). Both evaluated individual actions rather than character. The revival of virtue ethics began in the late twentieth century, driven by dissatisfaction with both.

Alasdair MacIntyre (1929–2025)

Scottish-American philosopher, for many years at the University of Notre Dame, whose landmark book After Virtue argued that modern moral philosophy had collapsed into fragmented, incommensurable viewpoints — what he called "emotivism," the view that moral statements are merely expressions of personal preference dressed up in objective-sounding language. Virtues, he contended, are intelligible only within the social practices and traditions that give them meaning.

MacIntyre's diagnosis hits close to home. When moral discourse migrates onto platforms optimized for engagement rather than truth, it becomes what he feared: a clash of incompatible emotional reactions, amplified by algorithms that reward outrage, with no shared framework for resolution. He writes that he "can only answer the question 'What am I to do?' if I can answer the prior question 'Of what story or stories do I find myself a part?'" Replacing narrative coherence with a feed of disconnected moments is, on this view, a threat to the preconditions of moral life.

The British-born New Zealand philosopher Rosalind Hursthouse answered a common objection: that virtue ethics fails to guide action. It generates "v-rules" — "Do what is honest," "Don't do what is cruel" — which derive their content from character rather than obligation and must be applied with practical wisdom. The virtuous person is not following a checklist; they can see what honesty requires here and now.

Martha Nussbaum developed the capabilities approach: flourishing requires the ability to exercise core human capacities — life; bodily health; senses, imagination, and thought; emotions; practical reason; affiliation; play; and control over one's environment. This connects virtue ethics to justice and policy, and gives us a checklist for asking whether social media helps or hinders.

Shannon Vallor

American philosopher of technology, since 2020 the Baillie Gifford Professor in the Ethics of Data and Artificial Intelligence at the University of Edinburgh, where she directs the Centre for Technomoral Futures. Her Technology and the Virtues is the most systematic application of Aristotelian ethics to digital technologies. Technology is not ethically neutral: through habitual use we cultivate either virtues or vices. She proposes twelve "technomoral virtues" for the digital age: honesty, self-control, humility, justice, courage, empathy, care, civility, flexibility, perspective, magnanimity, and technomoral wisdom. Character is still shaped by habituation, as Aristotle thought — but our habituating environment now includes billions of hours with systems designed for profit rather than flourishing.

A useful label for the ambiguity Vallor describes is the Greek pharmakon, meaning "poison" and "cure" at once — the word Plato's Socrates used for writing in the Phaedrus . Social media is not inherently vicious; it could be designed to cultivate patience, honest discourse, and genuine connection. But as presently constituted, Vallor argues, it habituates users toward vice. The question is not whether technology shapes our character — it already does — but whether we will take conscious control of how.

Thought Questions

  1. MacIntyre claimed you cannot answer "What am I to do?" without first knowing what story you are part of. What story does your social media presence tell about you, and did you choose it?
  2. Run Nussbaum's capabilities list — life, health, senses and thought, emotions, practical reason, affiliation, play, control over one's environment — against a platform you use daily. Which does it support, and which does it crowd out?
  3. Vallor's list of technomoral virtues includes flexibility, perspective, and magnanimity. Which virtue would you add to her twelve for the age of AI, and what practice would develop it?

Intellectual Virtues Under Threat

The intellectual virtues Aristotle prized — curiosity, love of truth, humility, open-mindedness, courage, autonomy — are not passive possessions but active dispositions that must be continuously exercised. One becomes intellectually humble by repeatedly confronting the limits of one's knowledge; curious by following questions wherever they lead. Like physical fitness, intellectual virtue atrophies without practice.

What's the Argument?

Argument 1: The Intellectual Vice Argument

  1. Intellectual virtues are cultivated through sustained, effortful cognitive practices: sustained attention, active memory work, critical analysis, and deep reading.
  2. Social media and AI systematically replace or undermine these practices.
  3. Habituation in intellectually passive behaviors cultivates intellectual vice rather than virtue.
∴ Social media and AI tend to undermine intellectual virtue.

The first premise is about how minds develop, the second an empirical claim about what technologies do, the third a restatement of Aristotle on habituation. Each is separately contestable, and this section examines them in turn — the honest way to hold an argument like this, not as a verdict but as commitments that can be checked.

The Attention Crisis

In The Shallows , journalist and technology critic Nicholas Carr argued that internet use is rewiring the brain for distraction. The case is not that the internet makes us less intelligent, but that habitual digital media use trains the mind toward shallow, rapid scanning at the expense of the sustained attention intellectual development requires. Deep reading — following complex arguments, inhabiting another perspective — requires holding attention over extended periods, and Carr argues that capacity is degraded by constant interruptions, hyperlinks, and notifications.

Jonathan Haidt's work on adolescent mental health adds a generational dimension. The shift to smartphones as the primary medium of teenage social life, which he dates to around 2012, coincided with rising reported depression and anxiety and with declines in book-reading and face-to-face talk. Whether these trends are causally connected is genuinely disputed; the eudaimonia section examines that dispute. The proposed mechanism is continuous partial attention: a chronic state in which no task receives full concentration because attention is always partly allocated to monitoring notifications. William James identified the capacity to return a wandering attention voluntarily as "the very root of judgment, character, and will" .

The 47-second figure is one of the most frequently mangled statistics in the debate. In observational studies of knowledge work, Mark's teams measured how long a person's focus stayed on one screen before shifting — screen-switching behavior, not the mind's capacity for concentration. Popular retellings convert it into "the human attention span has fallen to 47 seconds," a much stronger claim the research does not establish. The underlying finding still matters: a better-than-threefold increase in switching over two decades is a real change. But a virtue ethicist committed to the love of truth should be first to notice a statistic upgraded in transit. Treat phone-checking counts with the same caution — estimates range from roughly 60 to well over 150 a day depending on whether checking is self-reported or logged.

Outsourcing Memory and Cognition

A related concern involves what cognitive scientists call cognitive offloading — using technology as an external supplement to mental processes that were previously performed internally.

Cognitive offloading

Shifting part of a mental task onto something outside the mind — a note, a calculator, a search engine, a chatbot — so that the external resource carries work the brain would otherwise do.

Offloading is ancient and often sensible; a shopping list is cognitive offloading. What makes the digital case distinctive is scale and automaticity. The best-known evidence, from Betsy Sparrow and colleagues and described below, is that people who expect to look information up later are less likely to encode it. This "Google Effect" suggests that ready access to search may shift us from knowers who have internalized information toward retrievers who know how to locate it.

Case Study

The Google Effect Experiments (2011)

In July 2011, psychologists Betsy Sparrow, Jenny Liu, and Daniel Wegner published "Google Effects on Memory" in Science : four experiments testing whether knowing that a computer will remember something for you changes whether you remember it yourself.

In one, participants typed forty short trivia statements into a computer. Half were told the file would be saved; half that it would be erased. All were asked to remember them. Those who believed the information had been saved recalled significantly fewer, despite identical typing and instructions. In a further experiment, participants who saved each statement into a named folder recalled the folder better than the statement — memory reorganized around retrieval paths rather than content.

Two cautions: the effects were modest, and later replication work has been mixed, so treat this as suggestive rather than settled. Still, the mechanism is the one virtue ethics cares about — not that people became lazy, but that a reliable external memory silently changed what their minds bothered to do.

Carr notes that the medieval monks who invented many techniques of deep reading understood something important: committing texts to memory is not rote learning but a process that lets ideas combine and generate new thoughts. Stored externally, information cannot do this. And when AI tools compose arguments on our behalf, we lose not only the product of the effort but the character the effort was building.

Epistemic Bubbles and Echo Chambers

Philosopher C. Thi Nguyen draws a distinction easy to miss. An epistemic bubble is an environment in which you are not exposed to certain viewpoints — an information gap. An echo chamber is more insidious: outside voices have been actively discredited, so contrary evidence reinforces suspicion of the messenger instead of updating belief. Bubbles can be corrected by exposure; echo chambers cannot, because the mechanism for correction has been disabled.

Algorithmic curation creates bubbles by design. Someone who clicks a few videos skeptical of vaccine safety is served progressively more from the same cluster of creators, not because they are correct but because that engagement pattern predicts more engagement. They now "know" a great deal about vaccine skepticism and very little about the scientific literature. The echo chamber is the advanced stage — they have also learned to treat anyone citing mainstream sources as a paid shill.

AI and Intellectual Dependency

The most recent challenge comes from large language models. Vallor warns of "moral deskilling" through automation: the erosion of human capacities when machines take over tasks we once performed . The worry is analogous to the one about calculators but deeper. A calculator bypasses one specific skill; an AI that writes your argument may bypass the whole process of intellectual struggle through which analytical thinking develops.

Consider two students writing a philosophy essay. One spends an hour struggling to articulate Kant's categorical imperative, discarding three drafts, rereading the original, finally arriving at a formulation they can defend. The other types a prompt and receives a polished paragraph in ten seconds. The second has a product; the first has developed a capacity — and for a virtue ethicist the capacity, built through the struggle, is the whole point. Whether the concern is warranted is an empirical question that will take years to answer, but it is precisely the question a virtue ethicist would ask.

  1. When was the last time you read something complex for thirty or more minutes without checking your phone? Does the difficulty matter?
  2. Can you think of knowledge or skills you once had that you now rely on technology for? How does that feel?
  3. The "47 seconds" statistic is true of screen-switching and false as a claim about attention spans. What would you need to check before repeating a striking number you saw online?

Moral Virtues Under Threat

If intellectual virtues are developed through effortful cognitive practice, moral virtues are developed through the irreplaceable work of living alongside other people. Empathy develops through sustained exposure to others' vulnerability; patience through waiting and tolerating discomfort; honesty through repeatedly choosing truth over convenience. Consider what is lost when a difficult conversation that would once have happened face-to-face is conducted by text. The recipient does not see your hesitation or hear the tension in your voice; you do not have to sit with their pain. Both of you have been spared the discomfort that is, arguably, where moral learning happens.

What's the Argument?

Argument 2: The Moral Vice Argument

  1. Moral virtues are cultivated through embodied, face-to-face social practices involving vulnerability and reciprocity.
  2. Digital mediation systematically replaces or degrades these practices.
  3. Online environments actively habituate users toward moral vices: cruelty, impatience, dishonesty, and performative virtue.
∴ Social media and AI tend to undermine moral virtue.

The structure mirrors Argument 1, and so do its vulnerabilities. The third premise is the boldest: it says not merely that online life offers fewer chances to practice virtue, but that it supplies active practice in vice. That is the premise the rest of this section defends, and it is also the one an opponent should attack first.

Face-to-Face Conversation as Moral Practice

MIT sociologist Sherry Turkle has spent decades studying how people relate to technology. In Reclaiming Conversation , she argues that face-to-face conversation is irreplaceable as the context in which empathy develops. Speaking to someone in person, we receive a continuous stream of non-verbal information — expression, posture, tone, eye contact — that makes their inner life viscerally present. We learn to tolerate awkward silences and get immediate feedback when our words land wrong. These demands are uncomfortable, and avoiding them is exactly what text-based communication allows.

But the demands are, Turkle argues, the moral substance of relationship. "Face-to-face conversation is the most human — and humanizing — thing we do," she writes. "Fully present to one another, we learn to listen. It's where we develop the capacity for empathy." Teens who communicate more by text than in person practice a thinner form of interaction, one developing less empathy, patience, and social skill. There is some quantitative support: a cross-temporal meta-analysis by Sara Konrath and colleagues, pooling seventy-two samples of American college students who took the Interpersonal Reactivity Index between 1979 and 2009, found self-reported empathic concern and perspective-taking down roughly 40 percent, sharpest after 2000 . Two qualifications: these are self-reports, not observed behavior, and the data end in 2009, before smartphones were widespread — so the study cannot show smartphones caused the decline, only that something was already eroding self-reported empathy in the early internet era.

Online Disinhibition and the Normalization of Cruelty

Psychologist John Suler identified what he called the "online disinhibition effect" : the well-documented phenomenon that people behave more aggressively, dishonestly, and cruelly online than they would in person. He catalogued the features of online environments that produce this effect.

Suler's list is not a list of excuses; it is a list of the ordinary restraints that in-person life supplies and online environments remove. Online disinhibition is therefore not primarily a technical problem for content moderation but a moral habituation problem. Every time a person says something cruel online that they would not say to a face, they practice cruelty — and every cruel act, if Aristotle is right, shapes character in that direction, on an enormous scale.

Case Study

Justine Sacco and the Eleven-Hour Flight (December 2013)

On 20 December 2013, Justine Sacco, a corporate communications director at the media company IAC, posted a tasteless joke to her Twitter account before boarding an eleven-hour flight from London to Cape Town: "Going to Africa. Hope I don't get AIDS. Just kidding. I'm white!" She had 170 followers. Then she turned off her phone.

By the time she landed, #HasJustineLandedYet was the top trending topic worldwide. Thousands who had never heard of her spent the day composing jokes, calls for her firing, and — in many cases — wishes that she be raped or infected with HIV. One user went to Cape Town airport to photograph her arrival. She was fired, and the journalist Jon Ronson later devoted a chapter of So You've Been Publicly Shamed to reconstructing what happened to her and to the people who piled on .

What makes the case philosophically interesting is not the tweet, which almost everyone agrees was ugly. It is the pile-on. Ronson found that participants, interviewed afterward, were largely decent people who experienced the day as a satisfying act of collective justice. Every factor in Suler's table was in play: they could not see her, she could not answer, the crowd supplied cover, and the reward arrived immediately. This is what Aristotle's account predicts should be corrupting — not one monstrous choice by one monstrous person, but ordinary people getting a great deal of pleasurable practice at cruelty and calling it justice.

Outrage as a Business Model

Jonathan Haidt's research on social media's transformation of public discourse pinpoints 2009 as pivotal . That year Facebook added the Like button and Twitter the Retweet — small design choices that altered the incentive structure of online communication. Content generating strong emotional reactions, particularly anger and moral outrage, receives more engagement than nuanced content, and engagement is rewarded by algorithmic amplification. The cycle is self-reinforcing: emotional temperature rises, users are trained toward extremism, and what was once inflammatory becomes routine.

One name for what this rewards is moral grandstanding — a term introduced by the philosophers Justin Tosi and Brandon Warmke in 2016 for the use of public moral talk as a vehicle for self-promotion , and taken up by Haidt and others to describe what engagement-optimized platforms select for. The grandstander performs outrage as a social signal rather than acting on the problem: an hour spent sharing angry posts about an injustice counts as "doing something" while expending no effort that addresses it. On an Aristotelian view this is worse than inaction, habituating a person toward the performance of virtue while bypassing its practice.

Dishonesty and the Curated Self

Digital platforms also reward systematic inauthenticity. The curated Instagram profile — best photos, happiest moments, most flattering angles — creates a performed identity that diverges from lived experience, an ongoing dishonesty toward one's audience and oneself. Over time, Turkle argues in Alone Together , the performance can feel more real than the reality: people manage their lives to generate content rather than experiencing life directly.

Hannah Arendt warned that the erosion of a shared sense of reality is one of the preconditions of tyranny.

The ideal subject of totalitarian rule is not the convinced Nazi or the convinced Communist, but people for whom the distinction between fact and fiction and the distinction between true and false no longer exist.

Arendt was describing the 1930s and 1940s, not the internet, and the analogy should not be pressed too far. But her point was structural: tyranny does not require that everyone believe a particular lie, only that enough people stop believing the difference between true and false is worth maintaining. When deepfakes and strategic misinformation make it genuinely hard to know what is real, the democratic practices that depend on a shared factual world are threatened. The individual virtue of honesty has collective political stakes.

Thought Questions

  1. Have you ever written something online that you would not have said to someone's face? What made it easier?
  2. Think about your social media profile. In what ways does it represent who you actually are, and in what ways does it represent who you want to appear to be?
  3. Justine Sacco's tweet was genuinely offensive. Was there a proportionate response available to the thousands of people who saw it, and what would it have looked like?

Eudaimonia Under Threat

Beyond specific virtues, the deepest Aristotelian concern is the shape of a complete human life. Eudaimonia requires more than isolated virtuous acts: it requires the integrated exercise of our highest capacities across time, in the context of genuine relationships, purposeful activity, and honest self-knowledge. Social media and AI may be fragmenting attention, commodifying relationships, and distorting self-understanding in ways that threaten those conditions.

What's the Argument?

Argument 3: The Diminished Flourishing Argument

  1. Eudaimonia requires the integrated exercise of virtue across a complete life, including meaningful relationships, purposeful activity, and self-knowledge.
  2. Social media and AI fragment attention, commodify relationships, and distort self-understanding.
  3. These effects directly impede the conditions necessary for eudaimonia.
∴ Social media and AI tend to undermine human flourishing.

The Mental Health Crisis

The most widely discussed evidence for the eudaimonia argument is the rise in reported adolescent distress since roughly 2012. Haidt argues in The Anxious Generation that depression, anxiety, and self-harm among American teenagers — particularly girls — began climbing sharply around that year, close to the point at which smartphones became the majority technology among American teens. Social media, he proposes, enables comparison at a previously impossible scale: teenagers monitor hundreds of peers' curated self-presentations at once. Add phones displacing sleep and screens displacing face-to-face time, and you have conditions particularly corrosive for developing minds.

Public health authorities have taken the worry seriously without endorsing the causal claim. The 2023 United States Surgeon General's advisory concluded that the evidence of harm was sufficient to justify precaution and insufficient to establish that social media is safe — not the same as concluding it is the cause . This is the most contested empirical claim in the chapter, and a chapter organized around the intellectual virtues has to model what it recommends by laying out the strongest case against.

Objection

Critics including the developmental psychologist Candice Odgers and the researchers Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski argue that the causal claim outruns the evidence. Nearly all the underlying studies are correlational, and the correlations are small: in one large-dataset reanalysis, the association between digital technology use and adolescent well-being was comparable in size to associations with wearing glasses or eating potatoes . Reverse causation is at least as plausible — struggling teenagers may turn to their phones rather than being made to struggle by them. Rival explanations for the post-2012 rise must be beaten rather than assumed away: changing diagnostic and screening practice, reduced stigma around reporting, academic pressure, economic insecurity. Odgers's Nature review of The Anxious Generation puts it bluntly: the book tells a compelling story, but the science does not yet support the conclusion that social media is rewiring childhood .

Haidt's reply is that the skeptics look at the wrong evidence. Correlational studies of average daily use find small effects because the harm is concentrated and the measure crude; the better evidence comes from deactivation experiments and from the same inflection appearing in the same years across many countries. Critics respond that deactivation experiments are short, small, and inconsistent, and that international synchrony is what you would expect from any globally shared event .

Where does that leave the virtue ethicist? Aristotle held that eudaimonia requires conditions supporting human development, so how well those conditions are designed for young people is unavoidably an ethical question. But the answer depends partly on disputed empirical facts, and a chapter that quietly resolved the dispute in its own favor would practice the vice it warns against. The honest formulation: the smartphone hypothesis is serious, live, and unproven, and should be held provisionally.

Commodified Relationships and the Loneliness Epidemic

Alone Together captures a paradox: we are more "connected" than any humans in history, yet loneliness is now a public health problem rather than a private misfortune. The explanation lies in the difference between connection and genuine relationship. Social media friendship is measured by metrics — followers, likes, streaks — only approximately related to what makes friendship nourishing: shared vulnerability, mutual care, reciprocal knowledge of one another's inner lives.

Dating apps extend this commodification to romance, subjecting potential partners to rapid evaluation and easy disposal in ways that may undermine the patience deep intimacy requires. Parasocial relationships — the one-sided bonds users form with influencers, podcasters, and increasingly AI companions — offer the emotional texture of relationship without its reciprocal demands. Turkle worries that we have come to "expect more from technology and less from each other" , a substitution that erodes our capacity for the connection on which eudaimonia depends.

Algorithmic Identity and Distorted Self-Knowledge

Aristotle placed self-knowledge at the foundation of the ethical life. "Know thyself" was for him not a platitude but a demanding requirement: virtue requires perceiving our own motivations clearly, recognizing our tendencies toward excess and deficiency, and understanding the gap between who we are and who we aspire to be. That self-knowledge is threatened when our self-image is partly constructed by algorithms.

Recommendation algorithms construct a mirror image of you from your behavior patterns. But that image reflects what captures your attention, not what you reflectively endorse. The loop between your behavior, the algorithm's model, the content it feeds you, and your subsequent behavior can lock people into identities they would not have chosen — replacing the examined life with an optimized product built from the residue of a browsing history.

Thought Questions

  1. How many of your online connections would you describe as genuine friendships? What distinguishes a genuine friend from a follower?
  2. Have you ever felt that spending time on social media made you feel worse about your own life? What was the mechanism?
  3. Orben and Przybylski found that social media use predicts adolescent well-being about as strongly as wearing glasses does. Does that finding change your view, or do you think it is measuring the wrong thing?

Policy Responses

If the virtue ethics diagnosis is correct, what follows practically? Two broad categories of response are available: individual responses that change personal habits, and structural responses that address the design and regulation of technology at the systemic level. Virtue ethicists tend to see both as necessary, since willpower is limited and environmental design shapes what habits are even possible.

Table 2.6: Individual vs. Structural Responses to Technology's Moral Harms
Individual ResponsesStructural Responses
ExamplesPhone-free meals, digital sabbaths, attention trainingAge restrictions, design mandates, algorithm transparency
AdvantagesPersonal agency, immediately actionableAddresses root causes, applies equally
LimitsWillpower is exhaustible; advantage goes to tech firmsRaises paternalism concerns; difficult to enforce

The bottom row is where the interesting arguments live. Individual responses are limited because willpower runs out and the platforms do not; structural responses must survive both political objection and the problem of enforcement. Vallor calls individual responses — digital detoxes, attention training, commitments to face-to-face conversation, scrutiny of algorithmic recommendations — essential but insufficient: they cultivate the virtues being eroded, but require swimming against a powerful current of commercial incentives, which is why structural support is necessary.

Australia's Under-16 Ban: A Natural Experiment

Until recently, arguments about restricting children's access to social media were entirely hypothetical. That changed in December 2025, when Australia became the first country to put such a restriction into force nationwide — letting us watch a structural response meet the enforcement problem in public.

Case Study

Australia's Under-16 Social Media Ban (2024–2026)

The Online Safety Amendment (Social Media Minimum Age) Act 2024 passed the Australian Parliament in late November 2024 and took effect on 10 December 2025 . Its design detail matters: the legal duty falls entirely on platforms, which must take "reasonable steps" to prevent under-16s from holding accounts. No penalty attaches to a child or a parent. Ten services are covered, including Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat, TikTok, YouTube, X, and Reddit; WhatsApp, Messenger, Discord, and Roblox are not.

After roughly seven months the results were instructively mixed. Platforms removed or restricted more than five million under-16 accounts, yet eSafety's longitudinal data showed under-16 usage falling only from 85.9% to 81.5% . A survey of 1,050 Australian 12- to 15-year-olds by the Molly Rose Foundation found 61% of former account-holders still had access to at least one, 70% described getting around the ban as "easy," and 51% said it made no difference to their safety . Crucially, only 4–5% used a VPN: the workaround was social, not technical — a parent's ID, a login never signed out, a friend's account, a false birth date.

An April 2026 working paper from economists at the National Bureau of Economic Research, including Cass Sunstein, found fewer than one in three 14- and 15-year-olds complying at four months and roughly one in twenty two months later . Teenagers reported needing about two of every three peers to comply before they would — the classic structure of a tipping point — and those who did comply reported more boredom and more difficulty keeping up with friends.

Enforcement stayed quiet. No fines had been issued as of July 2026, though formal investigations into five major platforms opened in March 2026, and there was no clear decline in cyberbullying or image-abuse complaints. Public support among Australian adults nonetheless rose, and several other countries moved to copy the policy.

Read as a natural experiment, the case is more interesting than either side's talking points. It did not fail: five million accounts is not nothing, and a four-point drop in a behavior this entrenched is a real effect. It also did not do what it promised: four in five under-16s are still on social media, most who left could return tomorrow, and nobody has been fined. Three ethical questions follow.

First: does a law count as working if it is popular but unenforced? On one view an unenforced law is theatre — it lets a government claim to have acted while the harm continues, and teaches young people that rules are things you route around. On another, legislation also expresses a public norm, and norms shift slowly. The two-thirds tipping point suggests a law's real work may be to move a population toward that threshold rather than to punish individuals, in which case seven months is far too early to score it. Aristotle, who thought law's function was to habituate citizens rather than punish them, would have preferred this reading — though he would also have insisted that a law nobody obeys habituates nobody.

Second: is it fair that partial compliance mainly costs the students who obeyed? The teenagers who followed the rule reported more boredom and more difficulty keeping up with friends — because their friends had not. Where a restriction's benefit depends on nearly everyone accepting it, partial compliance delivers not a partial benefit but a pure cost, landing on exactly the children who did what was asked. The habituation is perverse: the policy gives compliant children practice at being penalized for compliance.

Third: who bears the privacy cost of mandatory age assurance? A platform cannot keep out under-16s without forming a view about how old everyone is — face-estimation software, document checks, or inferences from behavioral data, applied to the whole adult population to exclude a minority of children. The cost of protecting children's flourishing is thus paid partly by adults, in a currency (identity data) that Chapter 6 will show is hard to contain, and it falls hardest on those with the least documentation and the most reason to avoid identifying themselves. The Australian law softens this — platforms may not require government identification without offering an alternative — but no age-assurance technique is simultaneously accurate, cheap, and privacy-preserving.

Thought Questions

  1. Seven months in, Australian under-16 usage fell from 85.9% to 81.5%, and no platform has been fined. If you had to declare the law a success or a failure right now, which would you choose — and what evidence would change your mind?
  2. Public support for the ban rose to 77–79% among adults while only about 38% of Australian children supported it. Whose judgment should carry more weight on a policy about children's flourishing, and why?
  3. The children who complied reported more boredom and more social exclusion than those who did not. Does that make the law unjust, or does it make the case for enforcing it more strictly?

Other Structural Proposals

Other structural proposals include design mandates removing features that habituate users toward vice (infinite scroll, autoplay, manipulative notifications); algorithm transparency requirements, revealing what recommendation systems optimize for; digital literacy education; and AI governance frameworks that preserve rather than replace authentic human engagement. The virtue ethics justification is the same for all: we are entitled to design our shared environment to be more conducive to flourishing.

Thought Questions

  1. Which of Vallor's practical recommendations — digital sabbaths, attention training, face-to-face priority — could you realistically implement? What would it take?
  2. Design mandates would ban specific features rather than specific content. Is that a smaller intrusion on liberty than a content rule, or a larger one?
  3. Should platforms be required to disclose what their algorithms optimize for? Would knowing change how you use them?

Objections and Responses

The virtue ethics critique faces serious objections from several directions. Taking them seriously is itself an exercise in intellectual virtue.

Objection 1: The Paternalism Problem (Berlin and Mill)

  1. Virtue ethics assumes a particular conception of the good life — one centered on sustained attention, face-to-face connection, and active memory work.
  2. In a pluralistic society, the state should not impose any single conception of the good (Isaiah Berlin's negative liberty: freedom from interference, not freedom to flourish) .
  3. Restricting social media — or designing technology to cultivate particular virtues — treats adults as children unable to choose their own good.
  4. Therefore, virtue ethics arguments for technology regulation constitute paternalistic overreach.

The objection has two ancestors. John Stuart Mill's On Liberty insisted that "his own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant" for coercing a competent adult — the harm principle. Berlin's distinction is between negative liberty, the absence of external interference, and positive liberty, being free to realize your genuine potential. His concern, sharpened by bitter twentieth-century experience, was that appeals to what people's "true" interests require have historically justified some of history's worst tyrannies.

The objection is serious, but several responses are available. First, most virtue ethics policy proposals focus on children, and protecting the moral development of those without full autonomy is a much less controversial state interest. Second, the relevant choice may not be between free markets and intervention but between two forms of manipulation: platforms optimizing for engagement, and design oriented toward flourishing. If the environment already manipulates "choice architecture," the question is not whether to design it but how. Third, some interventions — transparency requirements, defaults rather than bans — change the environment of choice without eliminating options.

Objection 2: The Nietzschean Challenge — Who Decides What Virtue Is?

  1. Virtue ethics assumes we can reliably identify which character traits count as virtues and which as vices.
  2. But historically, "virtues" have often reflected power structures, cultural prejudices, or what Nietzsche called "herd mentality" — conformity to conventional expectations rather than genuine human excellence .
  3. Some characteristics labeled "vices" online — irreverence, confrontation, non-conformity — may be necessary for authentic excellence.
  4. Therefore, the virtue ethics critique of technology may reflect conservative cultural bias rather than genuine ethical insight.

Nietzsche's challenge is powerful. He argued that the virtues of the heroic aristocrat — great ambition and self-assertion — were suppressed in favor of "slave morality": the praise of meekness and conformity that reflects the needs of those who cannot dominate but can form majorities. In this light, the critique of social media might reflect the preferences of an intellectual class who value deep reading and sustained attention — a class whose practices happen to be threatened by digital populism.

The Aristotelian response appeals not to cultural consensus but to human nature: some traits are genuinely better for us because they more fully express our rational and social nature. Whether that fully satisfies is still debated. But Nietzsche's "higher man" presumably also requires the honesty and self-examination the critique aims to protect. Even on Nietzschean terms, technologies that cultivate self-deception, passivity, and comparison anxiety seem unlikely routes to genuine excellence.

A third objection is empirical, and this chapter has already conceded much of it: the correlations are small, reverse causation is live, and rival explanations for the post-2012 rise have not been ruled out. What the concession does not settle is what to do meanwhile. Australia acted on a disputed hypothesis and accepted the cost of being wrong; other governments waited and accepted a different cost. Neither is obviously the more rational response to uncertainty, and noticing that is more useful than pretending the evidence is stronger — or weaker — than it is.

Thought Questions

  1. Is there a meaningful difference between protecting children from social media and "protecting" adults? Where should that line be drawn?
  2. If the evidence that social media causes mental health harms is genuinely uncertain, how should policymakers respond — and does the burden of proof fall on those who want to regulate or on those who want to leave things alone?
  3. Mill would allow the state to interfere only to prevent harm to others. Can the case against engagement-optimized platforms be made entirely in terms of harm to others, without appealing to anyone's flourishing?

Key Points

  • Virtue ethics asks not "What should I do?" but "What kind of person am I becoming?" — making the habitual practices of digital life a central ethical concern.
  • Aristotle argued that virtues are stable traits developed through practice, so the environment in which we practice shapes the character we acquire.
  • Eudaimonia is not pleasure or life satisfaction but the active exercise of our highest capacities across a complete life.
  • Intellectual virtues may be undermined by the attention crisis, cognitive offloading, echo chambers, and AI dependency.
  • Moral virtues may be eroded when digital mediation replaces face-to-face practice, disinhibition normalizes cruelty, and outrage-optimizing algorithms reward moral performance over moral action.
  • The causal evidence is contested: Haidt's smartphone hypothesis about post-2012 adolescent distress is opposed by Odgers, Orben, and Przybylski, who find the correlations small and the causal question open, so it should be held seriously but provisionally.
  • Australia's under-16 ban (in force 10 December 2025) shows the limits of structural intervention: 5 million+ accounts removed, but usage fell only from 85.9% to 81.5%, most circumvention was social rather than technical, and no fines were issued.
  • Beware laundered statistics: the "47 seconds" figure measures time on one screen before switching, not attention span, and "we are what we repeatedly do" is Will Durant, not Aristotle.
  • Shannon Vallor argues that our habitual digital practices cultivate either virtues or vices, so intentional design for virtue is both possible and necessary — against which Berlin's negative liberty and Nietzsche's "who decides what counts as virtue?" remain the central challenges.
