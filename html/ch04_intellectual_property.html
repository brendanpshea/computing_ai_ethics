Introduction: What Is Intellectual Property?

Consider a thought experiment from an 1813 letter of Thomas Jefferson's. Light a candle from mine and you have fire, but my flame is no smaller. Give you half my sandwich and I have half as much. Physical goods are rivalrous — my use prevents yours. Ideas are not: share one and you can pass it to a hundred more people, and none of us has any less. If ideas cannot be "taken," what would it mean to "own" one?

He who receives an idea from me, receives instruction himself without lessening mine; as he who lights his taper at mine, receives light without darkening me.

Jefferson's puzzle is at the heart of intellectual property law — the rules granting creators exclusive rights over their creations for a set period. Unlike physical property rights, which protect what already exists, IP rights create legal scarcity where nature provides abundance. A book can be copied perfectly at near-zero cost; a drug formulation can be synthesized by any competent chemist. IP law manufactures the scarcity deliberately, on the argument that without it creators would have insufficient incentive to create.

Whether that argument is sound — whether IP systems serve their stated purpose, or have been captured by incumbents — is a central debate of this chapter. It matters for digital technology because computers are at their core copy machines, and networks distribute those copies globally for nothing. Nearly everything a computer does collides with the assumptions built into traditional IP law.

The Four Types of Intellectual Property

Modern IP law recognizes four categories, each with a different basis, duration, and set of rules. The differences matter because debates about IP often conflate them: arguments that make sense for patents may not apply to copyright.

Table 4.1 — The Four Types of Intellectual Property
Type What It Protects Duration (U.S.) Key Feature
Copyright Creative expression: books, music, films, software, art Life of author + 70 years Protects expression, not ideas; fair use exception
Patents Novel, useful, non-obvious inventions and processes 20 years from filing Requires public disclosure; exclusive right to make, sell, use
Trademarks Brand identifiers: names, logos, slogans Indefinite (if actively used and renewed) Prevents consumer confusion; can be lost through "genericide"
Trade Secrets Confidential business information: formulas, algorithms, client lists Indefinite (while kept secret) Ends if disclosed or independently discovered

Copyright matters most in the digital age, since it governs text, music, images, video, and software. Its crucial distinction is between ideas and expression: it protects the specific way an author expresses an idea, not the idea itself. Anyone may write a novel about a young wizard at a magical school; J.K. Rowling's copyright covers the words, characters, and plot of Harry Potter, not the concept of magical education. Ideas are the building blocks of all intellectual activity, and the law treats them as unownable.

Fair use

A doctrine in U.S. copyright law permitting limited unauthorized use of a protected work — for criticism, commentary, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, or parody. Courts decide case by case, weighing four statutory factors: the purpose and character of the use, the nature of the original, how much was used, and the effect on the market for the original.

Fair use is the pressure valve in the copyright system, and it is deliberately vague: no list of permitted uses, no bright line, only four factors a judge weighs after the fact. That vagueness is a feature if you think copyright must bend to circumstances its drafters could not foresee, and a bug if you are a documentary filmmaker trying to learn in advance whether you are about to be sued. Nearly every hard question in this chapter, from Google's use of Java to the training of language models, comes down to how a court weighs those factors.

Patents differ from copyright in requiring public disclosure. An inventor must explain how the invention works, and that explanation becomes public record; in exchange comes a twenty-year monopoly on making, selling, and using it. The theory is a fair bargain — society gets the knowledge, the inventor a temporary exclusive right, and afterwards the invention is free for anyone. Whether twenty years is the right price is a question the pharmaceutical case study below will sharpen.

A Brief History of Expanding Rights

Table 4.2 — Key Milestones in Intellectual Property History
Year Development
1710 Statute of Anne (Britain): first copyright law; 14-year term, renewable once
1790 U.S. Copyright Act: 14-year term, renewable once; narrow scope
1886 Berne Convention: international copyright harmonization; automatic protection
1976 U.S. Copyright Act: extended to life + 50 years; work-for-hire rules
1994 TRIPS Agreement: linked IP to global trade; forced developing countries to adopt strong IP regimes
1998 DMCA + Sonny Bono Act: extended to life + 70 years; criminalized circumvention of digital locks
2023–2026 Streaming and generative AI reshape every IP category at once; the first rulings on AI training arrive, and one AI company pays $1.5 billion to settle a class action

The striking feature of IP history is the relentless expansion of rights. U.S. copyright began at 14 years and now runs to the author's life plus 70; patents have expanded to cover software, business methods, and algorithms earlier courts would never have found patentable. Each extension was justified as "encouraging creativity," but that applies awkwardly to retroactive extensions: extending copyright on a work already created cannot incentivize its creation. Something else is driving the expansion.

Thought Questions

  1. Jefferson argued that sharing an idea doesn't diminish it. Does that make IP law unjust — or does non-rivalry affect only the appropriate length of protection?
  2. Copyright terms have expanded from 14 years to life + 70. What term would you choose designing the system from scratch, and what would guide the decision?
  3. The idea-expression distinction protects ideas from monopoly. Can you think of cases where the line is genuinely hard to draw?

Philosophical Justifications for IP

Why should intellectual property rights exist at all? Three traditions offer competing answers, each with different policy consequences. A Lockean thinks IP should track the labor invested; a utilitarian cares only whether the system produces more creation than the alternatives; a Hegelian thinks certain authorial rights are inalienable regardless of economics.

The Labor Argument

What's the Argument?

Locke's Labor Theory of Intellectual Property

  1. People have a natural right to the fruits of their labor — what they make by mixing their effort with the world belongs to them.
  2. Intellectual creations are the product of sustained mental labor: a novelist's years of effort, an inventor's decades of research.
  3. Without legal protection, others can copy the result of that labor without contributing to the effort that produced it.
∴ Creators have a natural right to control their intellectual creations, as they do the products of their physical labor.

Locke's argument, adapted from his Second Treatise of Government , tracks our everyday sense that people deserve credit for what they make. But ideas are never purely original. Newton "stood on the shoulders of giants"; Shakespeare borrowed his plots wholesale; virtually every song shares chord progressions and melodic conventions with countless predecessors. If creations always build on prior creations, how much of any particular work is genuinely the creator's own? The Lockean framework struggles to draw the line.

Locke added a proviso: appropriation is justified only so long as "enough and as good" is left for others. In the physical world that runs out — there is only so much land. In the world of ideas it never does, since Jefferson's having expressed an idea does not stop anyone else from expressing it in their own words. So even if the labor theory justifies some IP rights, it justifies none of the strongest forms, which prevent others from building on existing ideas.

The Incentive Argument

What's the Argument?

The Utilitarian Case for Intellectual Property

  1. Society benefits greatly from creative works and inventions — from drugs that save lives to novels that deepen human understanding.
  2. Creating such works requires substantial investment — a new drug can cost over a billion dollars to develop and test.
  3. Without protection, competitors can copy successful works and undercut creators on price without having borne the development cost.
  4. This free-rider problem would reduce creators' incentives and therefore the quantity of new creation.
∴ Temporary exclusive rights are justified because they maximize social welfare, creating incentives for valuable work that would otherwise be undersupplied.

The incentive argument is how IP is most commonly defended in courts, legislatures, and economics textbooks. It is also the most empirically testable, and the empirical picture is messier than the theory. In pharmaceuticals, where development cost vastly exceeds copying cost, patent protection does appear necessary to sustain investment in R&D. Elsewhere, industries have thrived for centuries without meaningful IP protection. Consider four of the most creatively dynamic fields in modern life, all operating with minimal IP protection:

  • Fashion design. Garment designs generally cannot be copyrighted in the United States, yet Paris houses have driven global trends for over a century, relying on prestige, lead time, and rapid iteration rather than legal exclusivity.
  • Restaurant cuisine. Recipes cannot be copyrighted, yet fine dining innovates continuously. Ferran Adrià's techniques at El Bulli were freely copied worldwide, and his restaurant remained the most coveted reservation on earth anyway.
  • Stand-up comedy. Once a joke is told publicly anyone can repeat it, and lawsuits are almost unheard of. Comedians keep producing new material, policed by an informal norm against joke theft.
  • Sports strategy. A coach who invents a new play cannot patent it; opponents can adopt it the following week. Coaching innovation continues unabated.

In none of these cases has the absence of IP stifled creation. It has forced creators to compete on ongoing innovation rather than past monopoly — on the utilitarian's own terms, not obviously worse. Open-source software, produced by volunteers with no IP-derived revenue, powers most of the world's servers and smartphones. IP incentives are neither universally necessary nor universally effective.

Term length sharpens the question: how much protection is enough? Economic analysis puts the optimal term far below current law. A songwriter is not more likely to compose because copyright will run seventy years after their death rather than fifty; those extra decades pay heirs and corporate rights-holders, not the creator. Current terms exceed what incentive theory justifies, which raises the question of whose interests the extensions serve.

The Personality Argument

What's the Argument?

Hegel's Personality Theory of Creative Rights

  1. Persons have dignity and deserve respect as self-creating beings whose identity is expressed in their projects and creations.
  2. Creative works embody the creator's personality and self-expression in a way that purely physical products do not.
∴ Creators have moral rights beyond economic interests — attribution (credit) and integrity (protection against distortion) — that persist even after commercial transfer.

The personality theory, associated with Hegel's Elements of the Philosophy of Right and developed most fully in continental European law, gives rise to moral rights — rights independent of economic interests that in many jurisdictions cannot be signed away. A French author who sells the copyright in a novel still retains the right to be named as its author and to object to alterations that damage their reputation. These rights appear in most European systems and in Article 6bis of the Berne Convention, but are much weaker in the United States, where copyright is predominantly a commercial instrument.

A revealing example: in 1975 ABC broadcast episodes of Monty Python's Flying Circus with roughly a quarter of the running time cut for commercials and for material thought offensive to American audiences. The troupe sued and, in Gilliam v. American Broadcasting Companies (2d Cir. 1976) , won a preliminary injunction. But the Second Circuit got there through copyright — the editing exceeded the license ABC had obtained — and through the Lanham Act's ban on false designation of origin, noting explicitly that American copyright does not recognize the droit moral. The troupe's complaint — this is no longer our work, yet it still bears our name — is exactly what moral rights address, and US law had to reach it sideways. A French author would have had a direct claim.

This has an implication for AI-generated content: if moral rights are grounded in the expression of a creator's personality, a work generated by an AI — which has no personality in the relevant sense — earns no such protection. We return to this below.

The Social Contract of IP

All three justifications share a structure: IP rights are a bargain between creators and the public. Creators receive a temporary exclusive right; in exchange, the public eventually receives full, free access. The bargain is explicit in patent law, which requires disclosure in exchange for the monopoly, and implicit in copyright, which expires. Working properly, it balances creator incentives against public access — neither so weak that creators cannot recoup their investment, nor so strong as to be a permanent monopoly on human knowledge.

Critics like James Boyle argue that the bargain has broken down . Terms have been extended so often that works effectively never enter the public domain within the lifetime of anyone alive at their creation. The result, Boyle argues, is a "second enclosure movement" — the fencing off of the intangible commons of the mind, analogous to the enclosure of English common lands.

Thought Questions

  1. Which justification — labor, incentive, or personality — do you find most compelling? Does your answer change for music copyright versus pharmaceutical patents?
  2. The incentive theory says IP should provide just enough protection to motivate creation. How would you test empirically whether a given term achieves that?
  3. If AI can generate creative content without any financial incentive, what does that imply for the incentive justification for copyright?

Digital Disruption

IP law was built on assumptions about the physical world: that copying is expensive and imperfect, that distribution requires infrastructure, and that enforcement is practical at the level of commercial reproduction. Digital technology invalidated all three at once. A perfect copy costs essentially nothing; the internet distributes it globally in seconds; and the volume of individual copying makes suing copyists impractical. The result was one of the most severe disruptions any established industry has experienced.

Thomas A. Edison, c. 1877–1880. Public domain.

Thomas Edison (1847–1931)

American inventor and businessman, Edison held 1,093 U.S. patents — a record that stood for decades. He pioneered the industrial research laboratory at Menlo Park, New Jersey, his "invention factory": researchers whose job was to produce patentable inventions on a schedule. His best-known work includes the phonograph, the practical incandescent light bulb, and early motion picture technology.

His career raises a persistent question about the patent system and innovation. The laboratory's output was remarkable. But his aggressive patent strategies — especially in the "War of Currents" against Nikola Tesla and George Westinghouse over the electrical power standard — are widely seen as having retarded adoption of the superior AC system. Edison used IP as a competitive weapon, not merely a reward for invention: a combination of creativity and strategic litigation characteristic of how large actors use IP today.

How Digital Changed Everything

Before digitization, the economics of creative industries rested on the cost of physical reproduction: a record required pressing, a book printing and binding, a film celluloid and a projector. Those constraints made large-scale copying impractical for ordinary consumers. Record labels, publishers, and film studios were not primarily in the business of creating content but of manufacturing and distributing objects, and their power came from controlling that infrastructure.

Digital technology severed content from carrier. A music file is not an object but a pattern of numbers, copyable perfectly at negligible cost. When Napster launched in 1999, college students were sharing millions of songs peer to peer, with no physical product, no money, and no license; industry revenue collapsed. The response — litigation, legislation, and technical protection measures — shaped the internet's legal landscape for decades.

Case Study

Napster and the P2P Revolution (1999–2001)

Launched by Shawn Fanning and Sean Parker in 1999, Napster let users share MP3 files through a centralized index server; at its peak it had over 80 million registered users. The Recording Industry Association of America and the major labels sued for contributory infringement in A&M Records v. Napster; the courts agreed, and Napster shut down in 2001.

But that did not end file sharing — it pushed it toward architectures with no central index to shut down. Kazaa, Grokster, and BitTorrent were harder targets precisely because there was less to sue. The industry kept trying: in MGM Studios v. Grokster (2005) the Supreme Court held unanimously that a service could be liable for inducing infringement, which finished Grokster but not the technology. Eventually the labels accepted that they could not sue their way out. Napster's lasting consequence was not the death of piracy but of the CD business model, replaced by streaming: the iTunes Music Store (2003) and Spotify (2008) showed consumers would pay for convenient legal access. By the 2020s streaming had restored the industry to growth — with value concentrated in platforms rather than labels or artists.

Case Study

The DMCA and the Problem of Digital Locks (1998)

The Digital Millennium Copyright Act was Congress's response to digital reproduction. Its most consequential provision was the anti-circumvention clause, which made it illegal to break "technological protection measures" (digital locks, or DRM) even for purposes otherwise entirely legal under copyright. The logic: a rights-holder's lock should itself be legally protected.

The consequences were broader than anyone anticipated. Security researchers reverse-engineering software for vulnerabilities found themselves potentially criminalized. Owners wanting to repair their tractors, insulin pumps, or smartphones found that accessing the embedded diagnostic software could violate the DMCA. Librarians making accessible versions of digital texts faced legal uncertainty. The EFF and others argue that the law transferred power from content owners to manufacturers — you can own a DVD but not legally back it up. Exemptions granted by the Copyright Office every three years provide limited relief, but the law remains a significant restriction on the ownership rights and fair use privileges copyright otherwise protects.

From Ownership to Access: The Streaming Model

The dominant response to digital disruption has been a shift from ownership to access. Under the old model consumers paid once to own a physical copy; under the new one they pay a monthly fee for a catalog they do not own and that can be modified or withdrawn at any time. The IP implications are easily overlooked in celebrations of streaming's convenience.

Table 4.3 — Streaming Economics: Who Gets What
Metric Value (approximate)
Spotify pay per stream $0.003–$0.005 (varies by country and deal terms)
Streams needed to earn $1 200–330 streams
Minimum payout threshold Since 2024, a track must reach 1,000 streams in a year to earn anything at all
Who captures most streaming revenue Platforms and major labels (through negotiated catalog deals)

Streaming reveals a gap between formal IP rights and practical economic power. An independent musician owns the copyright to their recordings but, to reach listeners, must accept terms set by a near-monopolist platform negotiating with dispersed individual creators. The right is present; the bargaining power to extract value from it is not. That pattern — rights in creators, power in platforms — recurs across the creative economy and drives the critiques in the next section.

Thought Questions

  1. Did Napster "kill" the music industry, or force it toward a better business model? Could the industry have made that transition without the disruption?
  2. The DMCA bars circumventing digital locks even for legal purposes. Is that a reasonable balance between creator and user rights, or has it tilted too far?
  3. A streaming subscription rents access rather than conferring ownership. Does the distinction matter morally? What are its practical consequences?

Critiques and Abuses of IP

A significant body of scholarship argues that IP law, whatever its original justifications, has become a mechanism for concentrating power in incumbent corporations rather than rewarding genuine creation. Three scholars have been especially influential.

Peter Drahos (b. 1955)

Australian legal scholar at the Australian National University, Drahos analyzes how IP has been globalized as a tool of corporate and geopolitical power. His central concept, developed with the criminologist John Braithwaite, is "information feudalism" : through trade agreements like TRIPS (Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights), a few American and European pharmaceutical, software, and entertainment corporations pressed governments worldwide to adopt strong IP regimes — including developing countries with no domestic interest in them.

The mechanism was leverage: IP protection was bundled with access to wealthy-country markets, so countries that wanted trade had to accept terms written by their partners' corporate lobbies. The result is a set of global norms designed to maximize rents to incumbent rights-holders, not to balance incentive against access. Drahos calls this "IP feudalism" because it creates hierarchies between owners (lords) and those who must pay for access to medicines, seeds, and software (serfs).

Cory Doctorow (b. 1971)

Canadian-British science fiction writer and technology journalist, Doctorow is among the most accessible critics of how Big Tech and Big Content use IP to maintain market power. His central concept, developed with the legal scholar Rebecca Giblin, is "chokepoint capitalism" : Amazon, Spotify, Google, Apple, and the content conglomerates have created bottlenecks in markets for creative goods, and IP law plus platform power lets them squeeze creators from both sides.

He distinguishes IP's original purpose — giving creators enough leverage to earn a living — from its current function of giving platforms tools to extract rents from creators and consumers alike. The problem is not piracy but monopsony: creators face a few buyers with enormous power, while those buyers face many sellers with no individual leverage. IP law here doesn't protect creators; it protects platforms' control over the terms on which creators reach audiences.

Tim Wu (b. 1972)

American legal scholar, former White House adviser, and originator of "net neutrality," Wu has analyzed how communication technologies follow a predictable cycle in their relationship to power . His "Cycle" runs: open technology attracts users → incumbents use IP and other legal tools to close it → consolidation follows → until the next disruptive technology reopens the market.

IP, Wu argues, is a primary instrument of the "closing" phase. AT&T used patents to control the telephone network for decades; Hollywood used copyright to control film distribution; IBM used software patents to hold mainframe dominance. The rights were real and valid, but their function was to maintain incumbent control rather than reward innovation. Big Tech's current dominance is one more iteration — one that IP law lets persist far longer than it otherwise would.

Case Studies in IP Abuse

The clearest illustration is Mickey Mouse. Mickey first appeared in "Steamboat Willie" in 1928, a work that under the law of the time would have entered the public domain in the 1980s. Disney lobbied successfully for extensions in 1976 and again in 1998 — the latter often called the "Sonny Bono Act" or, sardonically, the "Mickey Mouse Protection Act" — each time just before Mickey would have gone free. Steamboat Willie finally entered the public domain on January 1, 2024. Disney retains trademark protection on the modern Mickey, so even the public-domain version cannot be used in ways that confuse consumers about Disney's brand.

A second abuse is the "patent troll" (the industry's term is "non-practicing entity"): a company that owns patents but makes no products. Its model is to acquire patents — often vaguely worded ones covering broad concepts — and threaten companies that do make things, extracting settlements cheaper than the cost of defense. A patent suit costs two to five million dollars to defend through trial, so settling for $100,000–$500,000 is rational even for a defendant who would win. Personal Audio LLC famously sued podcasters over a patent on "episodic content distribution"; the Electronic Frontier Foundation eventually invalidated it, but only after years of litigation. Economists estimate trolls extract billions annually, most of it going to lawyers and shareholders rather than any inventor, with small startups hit hardest.

Case Study

Pharmaceutical Patents and Access to Essential Medicines

Pharmaceuticals present the starkest version of the tension between IP incentives and public welfare. Drug development is genuinely expensive: a new drug from research to FDA approval costs an estimated one to two billion dollars, and most compounds entering clinical trials fail. Twenty years of patent protection lets companies recover those costs by charging far above production cost; after expiration, generics sell for a fraction of the price.

The human cost is stark. When HIV/AIDS antiretrovirals were first patented in the 1990s they were priced at $10,000–$15,000 per year — far beyond the reach of patients in sub-Saharan Africa, where the epidemic was worst. Generic versions, when permitted, cost around $100 per year. Insulin costs several times more in the United States than in other wealthy countries because of patent and market strategies. The list price of an EpiPen two-pack rose roughly sixfold between 2007 and 2016, from under $100 to more than $600, even though epinephrine has been off patent for decades; what Mylan controlled was the auto-injector device and the regulatory pathway a competitor would have to clear. These cases raise a serious question about whether the patent bargain is well-calibrated to the public interest here.

Thought Questions

  1. Retroactive extensions like those protecting Mickey Mouse cannot incentivize work already done. What best explains why they keep happening?
  2. Pharmaceutical companies argue that without patents they could not afford to develop new drugs. Is that convincing? What alternative models might work?
  3. Patent trolls use legally valid patents abusively. Is the problem with specific actors, or with the system that enables them?

Big Tech and IP as a Competitive Weapon

Large technology companies use intellectual property not primarily to recover investment costs but as a strategic instrument of competitive advantage — a conception of IP as corporate strategy rather than as a reward for creators.

Table 4.4 — How Big Tech Deploys IP Strategically
IP Tool Strategic Use
Patent portfolios Build defensive portfolios; cross-license with other giants; sue smaller competitors who cannot reciprocate
Copyright Control user-generated content; issue DMCA takedowns against critics and competitors; protect proprietary code
Trade secrets Keep algorithms, training datasets, and business methods confidential, even in litigation
Trademarks Brand protection; control keyword advertising; block competitors' use of similar names
Terms of Service Create IP-like control over user behavior without the limitations that formal IP law imposes

One pattern is "mutually assured IP destruction." Google, Apple, Microsoft, and Amazon each hold tens of thousands of patents, so any one that sued another could expect an immediate counterclaim. The result is a cold war in which the giants cross-license implicitly, by threat alone. The détente does not extend to smaller competitors, who lack the portfolio to retaliate; for them, patent litigation from a well-resourced incumbent is a serious barrier to entry — which is, of course, the point.

Terms of Service as Private Law

Terms of Service agreements function as private IP law. A typical ToS grants the platform a perpetual, worldwide, royalty-free license to use, display, and distribute anything the user uploads; lets it remove content at will and change the agreement unilaterally; and requires arbitration rather than court. These provisions create IP-like control over user content without copyright's limitations: no fair use, no term limit, no public domain. And they are contracts of adhesion, signed without negotiation by users who often have no alternative.

Case Study

Oracle v. Google and the Question of API Copyright (2021)

An Application Programming Interface (API) is the set of function calls and definitions that let software systems interact. Google used Java APIs — the declarations of function names and signatures — in building Android, without licensing them from Oracle, which had acquired Java from Sun Microsystems. Oracle sued for billions. The case turned on a question at the heart of the software industry: can API declarations be copyrighted?

After more than a decade of litigation, the Supreme Court ruled 6–2 for Google in 2021 — but on fair use grounds, without deciding whether APIs are copyrightable at all . The Court emphasized the transformative nature of Android's use and the programmer investment in learning Java's APIs. Had Oracle won, incumbents with established APIs could have locked competitors out of interoperable software. IP boundaries, the case shows, determine whether software markets stay competitive or get captured by first-movers.

The same logic runs through the right-to-repair fight. Buy a modern iPhone, John Deere tractor, or hospital medical device and you get the object without full rights over it: manufacturers combine DMCA anti-circumvention provisions, parts pairing (software that rejects components the manufacturer has not authenticated), and proprietary diagnostics to make independent repair impossible or illegal. A farmer locked out of a tractor's diagnostics may wait days for an authorized technician instead of fixing the problem in hours. If you cannot repair, modify, or resell a product freely, in what sense do you own it? Since around 2020 several states have passed right-to-repair laws and some manufacturers have loosened restrictions, but the tension is unresolved.

Thought Questions

  1. If APIs could be copyrighted with no fair use defense, how would the software industry change? Would innovation gain or suffer?
  2. Do you "own" a device you cannot legally repair? What moral intuitions does the right-to-repair debate activate?
  3. Terms of Service grant platforms sweeping rights over user content in exchange for access. Is that a fair exchange? Should ToS be regulated?

AI and Intellectual Property

Artificial intelligence creates two clusters of IP problems existing law is ill-equipped to handle: whether training on copyrighted works infringes, and who — if anyone — owns the copyright in what an AI generates. Public discussion runs them together, but they are separate questions with separate answers, and as of 2026 one is settled while the other is very much not.

Training Data and the Copyright Question

Large language models are trained on massive collections of text, including enormous quantities of copyrighted books, articles, and academic papers; image models on billions of images, including the work of living artists. The legal question is whether copying protected works in order to extract statistical patterns from them infringes. That question contains several separate questions, which courts have answered differently.

Table 4.5 — Competing Positions on AI Training and Copyright
Position Core Argument
Infringement Training copies entire works; models can reproduce copyrighted content; creators' work was used without consent or compensation to build commercial products
Fair use Training is transformative (pattern extraction, not reproduction); models don't substitute for the originals; analogous to Google Books, held fair use
New paradigm needed Copyright was not designed for machine learning; a new licensing or levy framework is needed instead

Case Study

The AI Copyright Wars (2023–2026)

Beginning in 2023, a wave of copyright lawsuits landed on the major AI companies. New York Times v. OpenAI and Microsoft alleged that OpenAI had trained on millions of Times articles and that ChatGPT could reproduce passages almost verbatim, in competition with the Times's own products. Authors, artists, and music publishers filed class actions against Meta, Anthropic, Stability AI, and others.

Three years on, the striking thing is how little has been settled. The dozen-plus suits against OpenAI and Microsoft were consolidated into one multidistrict litigation, which as of mid-2026 has produced no ruling on fair use and no settlement. Anthropic paid $1.5 billion to end one class action, but a settlement produces no precedent. And no federal appeals court has yet decided whether training an AI model on copyrighted work is fair use. The first to take up the question, the Third Circuit in Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence, heard argument on 11 June 2026 and has not ruled. There is no Supreme Court decision, and none pending.

The stakes are easy to state and hard to resolve. If training requires a license, only the best-capitalized firms can build frontier models. If training is broadly fair use, creators have little recourse when their work becomes raw material for systems that compete with them. Almost nothing here is binding precedent yet; expect the ground to move while you read.

What the Courts Have Actually Held

It is commonly said that "the courts ruled AI training is fair use." That summary is too coarse to be useful, and in one respect simply wrong. The most consequential decision so far, Bartz v. Anthropic (N.D. Cal., 23 June 2025) , delivered no single verdict on "AI training" at all. Judge William Alsup pulled the company's conduct apart into three distinct acts and evaluated each separately.

Table 4.6 — Bartz v. Anthropic (2025): one lawsuit, three different answers
Conduct Holding Reasoning
Training the model on the books Fair use "Exceedingly transformative" — the model learns statistical structure rather than reproducing the works
Destructively scanning lawfully purchased print books Fair use A format shift: one copy in, one copy out, nothing added to the number of copies in the world
Downloading and keeping 7 million-plus pirated books Not fair use Building a permanent library from pirate sources is not excused by the eventual transformative purpose

Anthropic settled the third claim rather than take it to trial. The settlement — $1.5 billion plus interest, roughly 482,000 titles at about $3,000 per work, some four times the $750 statutory minimum — received final approval on 20 July 2026 and is the largest copyright class settlement in United States history. Notice what it was for. Not for training a model, which the court had already held to be fair use. For downloading and keeping the books in the first place.

This is the most useful thing to carry away: the fight is turning less on what models learn than on how the training corpus was acquired. A company that buys books, scans them, and trains on the results is on reasonably firm ground. One that torrents a shadow library to avoid the purchase price is not — and Anthropic's shortcut cost vastly more than the books would have. Note the irony: because the case settled, Alsup's reasoning was never tested on appeal. It is a district court opinion that binds nobody.

Market dilution

The claim that a flood of machine-generated works can damage the market for human-made works of the same kind — not by copying any particular passage, but by saturating the market that the originals compete in.

Market dilution is the theory to watch, and the case that introduced it is routinely reported backwards. In Kadrey v. Meta (25 June 2025) , Judge Vince Chhabria granted Meta summary judgment on the training claim brought by thirteen authors, and coverage treated this as a second blessing for AI training. What Chhabria wrote was narrower: these plaintiffs lost on this record, having produced no evidence that Meta's models diluted the market for their books. He went out of his way to say that market dilution is a legitimate way of arguing the fourth fair-use factor — effect on the market — and that a better-prepared plaintiff could well win on it. A separate claim about Meta's use of BitTorrent, which uploads files while downloading them, remains live. Again acquisition outlives training.

The one case where a fair-use defense has actually failed at trial level is Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence (D. Del., 11 February 2025) , where Judge Stephanos Bibas held that Ross had infringed 2,243 Westlaw headnotes in building a legal research tool. Two features limit its reach: Ross's system was not generative — it retrieved existing judicial language rather than producing new text — and it competed directly with the product it learned from, which makes the fourth factor easy for the plaintiff. Ross appealed, and the Third Circuit's decision will be the first appellate word on any of this.

Outside the United States, the first judgment on generative-AI training resolved remarkably little. In Getty Images v. Stability AI ([2025] EWHC 2863 (Ch), 4 November 2025) , the English High Court took up Getty's claim that Stability had trained Stable Diffusion on its photo library. Getty largely lost: it abandoned the training and copyright claims mid-trial, mostly because the training happened outside the United Kingdom and lay beyond the court's reach, and its remaining secondary-infringement claim failed on a consequential point — a model's weights are not an "infringing copy," because they do not store the images. Getty was left with narrow trade mark findings. For a case billed as the definitive test of AI training, it settled almost nothing about training.

Thought Questions

  1. Bartz treated buying-then-scanning and torrenting differently, though the resulting model is identical. Is that principled, or a convenient way to punish something without confronting the harder question?
  2. Market dilution says a flood of AI-generated work can harm authors even when no sentence is copied. Is that a copyright harm — or ordinary competition, which copyright has never protected anyone against?
  3. Very little here is settled law. How much weight should a headline like "court rules AI training is legal" carry? What would you want to know first?

AI-Generated Content: The Authorship Question

The second cluster concerns what AI systems generate. If a language model writes a story, or a diffusion model produces an image, who — if anyone — holds the copyright? Here, unlike the training question, American law has a settled answer. The Copyright Office has long held that copyright requires a human author, and in Thaler v. Perlmutter the D.C. Circuit agreed, holding in March 2025 that the Copyright Act's "author" must be a human being . The Supreme Court denied review on 2 March 2026. Human authorship is no longer an open question.

What that means in practice was spelled out in Part 2 of the Copyright Office's report on copyright and artificial intelligence, published 29 January 2025 . Purely AI-generated output is not copyrightable. Where a human exercises genuine creative control — selecting, arranging, and modifying AI output — those contributions may be protected, assessed case by case. The line that surprises students most: prompts alone do not confer authorship, however long and detailed they are. A prompt describes a desired result but does not determine the expressive choices the system makes in reaching it.

The companion report on training, Part 3, is a different matter: released 9 May 2025 as a "pre-publication" version, it still has not been finalized . Its central claim counterweights confident assertions in either direction — fair use for AI training cannot simply be presumed. Commercial use of vast troves of copyrighted work to generate competing content goes beyond established fair use, the Office argued, "especially where this is accomplished through illegal access." That is the Office's view, not a court's holding — but it is the distinction Bartz drew, arrived at independently.

The rule has practical consequences. Purely AI-generated content effectively enters the public domain the moment it is made, so a company publishing such content has reason to obscure the AI's role in order to claim protection, leaving courts to decide how much human input is "enough." The Hegelian tradition suggests a deeper rationale: if moral rights are grounded in the expression of a creator's self, a work a human selects, directs, and repeatedly refines has a stronger claim than one produced with a single prompt. That is roughly where the Copyright Office landed, by way of American case law rather than German idealism.

Thought Questions

  1. AI-generated works are not copyrightable because copyright requires human authorship. Is that the right line — or should protection track creative effort, human or machine?
  2. If you spend three hours refining a prompt and a friend spends three hours editing a photograph, why should only one of you get a copyright? Make the best case for the Copyright Office's answer, then against it.
  3. Because purely AI-generated work is uncopyrightable, creators have an incentive to conceal how much AI they used. Is that a reason to change the rule, or to require disclosure?

Open Source and Alternative Models

The most important challenge to the incentive justification for IP is not an argument but a fact: enormous quantities of high-quality creative and technical work are produced outside the IP system entirely, through voluntary collaboration. Open source software is the most striking example, but not the only one.

The Open Source Movement

Linux — which runs most of the world's web servers, Android on billions of smartphones, and much of the internet's infrastructure — was not created by a corporation seeking to profit from IP rights. Linus Torvalds began it as a personal project in 1991, and thousands of volunteers worldwide have developed it since with no IP-derived revenue. Visit a typical website and nearly every layer between you and the content is open source: the Linux server, the Nginx process routing your request, the PostgreSQL database, the Python generating the page, the Chromium-based browser rendering it. Much of that stack was written by people who chose to give it away — some unpaid volunteers, some employees of firms that found sharing infrastructure cheaper than building it privately. Either way, exclusivity was not the motivating mechanism.

The open source model uses IP law itself to ensure openness. "Copyleft" licenses like the GNU General Public License grant everyone the right to use, modify, and redistribute the software — but require any modified version to carry the same license. This "viral" property means no one can take a copyleft project, modify it, and redistribute it as proprietary software: the moment you distribute, you must share your changes. "Permissive" licenses like MIT and Apache allow code into proprietary products. Both use copyright to achieve goals opposite to its traditional function.

All of this challenges the utilitarian justification. If IP incentives are necessary for high-quality creation, how do we explain Linux, Wikipedia, and the thousands of other projects that form the de facto infrastructure of the internet? Researchers studying these communities find a mix of motives: professional reputation, community belonging, ideological commitment to open knowledge, and the practical benefit of improving tools one uses oneself. None require exclusivity.

Creative Commons

Founded in 2001 by Lawrence Lessig and others, Creative Commons provides standardized licenses for non-software creative content. Where traditional copyright is all-or-nothing, CC licenses let creators grant specific permissions in advance, spanning a spectrum from nearly public domain to only slightly looser than full copyright.

Table 4.7 — Creative Commons License Types
License What It Allows
CC0 Anything at all — equivalent to placing the work in the public domain
CC BY Any use with attribution to the creator
CC BY-SA Any use with attribution + share alike (derivatives must use same license)
CC BY-NC Non-commercial use with attribution
CC BY-NC-ND Non-commercial use, no derivatives, attribution required

Wikipedia is the most prominent example: its content is CC BY-SA, so anyone can use, translate, or adapt its articles provided they attribute Wikipedia and license derivatives on the same terms. OpenStreetMap, Khan Academy, and most open educational resources use CC licenses too. These projects form an intellectual commons that the traditional IP system does not easily accommodate.

The AI era has complicated this. Many image generators and language models were trained on CC-licensed content, including images from Flickr and text from Wikipedia. Whether that matches the spirit, if not the letter, of licenses chosen to enable sharing is a live debate: creators who picked CC licenses to enable human adaptation may feel that training a commercial model on their work without compensation is a use they never authorized, even where the license text permits it.

Thought Questions

  1. If vast quantities of software, encyclopedic knowledge, and educational content are produced without IP incentives, does that undermine the utilitarian case for strong protection — or are these exceptional cases that don't generalize?
  2. The GPL uses copyright to enforce openness. Is that a clever hack turning IP against itself, or a misuse of a system built for a different purpose?
  3. If you release a work under a CC license and an AI company trains a commercial model on it, have your rights been violated? Should CC licenses address this explicitly?

Key Points

Key Points

  • Intellectual property differs from physical property because ideas are non-rivalrous, so IP law must create artificial scarcity by legal rule rather than rely on natural limits.
  • Three justifications compete: Locke's labor theory, the utilitarian incentive argument, and Hegel's personality theory, which grounds moral rights beyond economic ones.
  • IP terms have expanded continuously — U.S. copyright from 14 years to life + 70 — well past what any incentive-based justification supports.
  • Digital technology invalidated IP law's core assumptions at once, forcing a painful adaptation that shifted economic value from creators to platforms.
  • The DMCA's anti-circumvention provision criminalized breaking digital locks even for legal purposes, blocking repair, security research, and accessibility.
  • Critical scholars — Drahos, Doctorow, Wu — argue that IP has been captured by incumbents who use it to extract rents and foreclose competition rather than to incentivize creation.
  • AI training and copyright remain unsettled: no federal appeals court has ruled on whether training a model on copyrighted work is fair use, the first such case was argued in June 2026 and is undecided, and no Supreme Court decision exists or is pending.
  • Bartz v. Anthropic split the conduct rather than ruling on "AI training": training the model and scanning lawfully purchased books were fair use, keeping over seven million pirated books was not — and that last act produced the $1.5 billion settlement; because the case settled, the ruling binds nobody.
  • The decisive question is increasingly acquisition, not learning; a second theory to watch is market dilution, endorsed as valid in Kadrey v. Meta even though those plaintiffs failed to prove it on that record.
  • AI-generated content is not copyrightable because copyright requires a human author — settled by Thaler v. Perlmutter, cert denied March 2026 — and prompts alone, however detailed, do not make you an author.
  • Open source and Creative Commons show that high-quality creative and technical work can be produced outside IP incentive structures, challenging the universality of the utilitarian argument.
