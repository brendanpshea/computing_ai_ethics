Part I: The Definitional Challenge

"Artificial intelligence" is one of the most widely used — and most poorly defined — terms in contemporary discourse. Politicians promise to regulate it; companies compete to claim they have it; researchers argue about whether any current system actually achieves it. Before the ethical questions, then, a prior one: what are we actually talking about?

The difficulty is not merely semantic. How we define AI shapes what we build, how we evaluate it, what risks we take seriously, and who we hold responsible when things go wrong. A system described as "just software" invites one set of ethical questions; "an intelligent agent" or "potentially conscious" invites entirely different ones — with real consequences for funding, regulation, and public trust.

The Moving Goalposts Problem

A well-known pattern in the history of AI is sometimes called Tesler's Theorem after computer scientist Larry Tesler: "AI is whatever hasn't been done yet." (Tesler's own wording was "Intelligence is whatever machines haven't done yet"; the snappier version, popularized by Douglas Hofstadter, is the one that stuck.) Whenever an AI system succeeds at a task once thought to require genuine intelligence, that task tends to get reclassified as "mere computation."

Table 7.1 — The Moving Goalposts: Technologies Once Called AI
Technology Once Called Now Called
Chess playing (Deep Blue, 1997) Artificial Intelligence "Just search algorithms"
Speech recognition AI breakthrough "Just statistics"
Spam filtering Machine learning "Just classification"
Route optimization (GPS) Intelligent systems "Just algorithms"
Optical character recognition Pattern recognition AI Standard software

The paradox embedded in this pattern is philosophically significant. It suggests that "AI" functions less as a stable category and more as a placeholder for unsolved problems. When we solve them, we discover — or decide — that we never had genuine intelligence to begin with. This makes "artificial intelligence" nearly impossible to achieve by definition: success disqualifies. Understanding this pattern helps explain why debates about whether current large language models are "truly" intelligent are so heated and often so unresolvable.

Tesler's Theorem

"AI is whatever hasn't been done yet." Once an AI system successfully performs a task, that task is retroactively reclassified as "mere computation" — not genuine intelligence. The goalposts move with every success, making AI an ever-retreating horizon.

A Taxonomy of AI Distinctions

Contemporary AI discourse employs several overlapping distinctions, each of which carries different philosophical commitments and practical implications. Understanding them is essential to navigating debates about AI capabilities and ethics.

Table 7.2 — Core Distinctions in AI Discourse
Distinction First Term Second Term
Narrow vs. General Excels at one specific task (chess, translation, image recognition) Performs any intellectual task a human can (AGI)
Weak vs. Strong Simulates intelligent behavior without possessing a mind Actually has genuine mental states — understanding, consciousness
Symbolic vs. Connectionist Rule-based reasoning; explicit knowledge representation (GOFAI) Neural networks that learn patterns from data
Tool vs. Agent Used by humans to perform specific tasks Acts autonomously toward goals in an environment

Most current AI systems are narrow (they excel at specific tasks but fail badly outside them), weak in Searle's sense (they simulate intelligent behavior without anyone seriously claiming they have minds), connectionist (based on neural networks trained on data rather than hand-coded rules), and increasingly agent-like (capable of taking sequences of actions toward goals, not just responding to single queries). These characteristics interact: a narrow, connectionist system that acts agentically in a constrained domain raises different ethical questions than one that might approach general capability.

Thought Questions

  1. Tesler's Theorem says "AI is whatever hasn't been done yet." Do you find this pattern troubling, or does it show that "AI" is just a name for problems at the frontier of computing — and that's fine?
  2. Pick one piece of software you used today. Which side of each distinction in Table 7.2 does it fall on? Were any of the four distinctions hard to apply, and what does that difficulty tell you?
  3. Steelman the opposite view: make the strongest case that "artificial intelligence" is a marketing term with no stable content, and that we should stop using it in serious argument altogether.

Part II: A History of AI Technologies

Artificial intelligence did not arrive suddenly with ChatGPT. It has a rich and humbling history — a story of genuine breakthroughs, spectacular overpromises, funding collapses, and patient rebuilding. Understanding this history reveals not just how current AI came to be, but recurring patterns about what happens when a technology fails to meet expectations and what it takes to eventually deliver on its promises.

Prehistory: Dreams of Artificial Minds

The dream of artificial intelligence is ancient. Greek mythology featured Hephaestus, the blacksmith god, who forged golden robots to assist him in his workshop. Medieval engineers built elaborate clockwork automata — mechanical figures that could mime human actions. Descartes speculated that animals might be nothing more than very sophisticated machines. What these visions share is a persistent intuition that intelligence might be separable from biology — that the right arrangement of parts could produce the right behavior.

The modern story begins in the seventeenth century with Leibniz's vision of a calculus ratiocinator — a machine that could perform logical reasoning and resolve disputes by calculation rather than argument. Two centuries later, Charles Babbage designed the Analytical Engine (1837), a mechanical general-purpose computer that was never built in his lifetime. It was in notes on this machine that the first serious philosophical argument about machine intelligence appeared.

Key Thinker

Ada Lovelace (1815–1852)

Ada Lovelace — daughter of the poet Lord Byron, educated in mathematics by her mother to counteract what her mother feared were dangerous poetic tendencies — collaborated with Charles Babbage on his Analytical Engine in the 1840s. Her 1843 notes on the machine, published under her initials "A.A.L.," contain what is widely regarded as the first computer algorithm. She also foresaw that such a machine could manipulate symbols of any kind — not just numbers — anticipating the generality of modern computers. The Ada programming language, developed for the US Department of Defense, was named in her honor in 1979; its 1980 military standard was numbered MIL-STD-1815 after her year of birth.

But Lovelace is equally important for what she argued a machine could not do. Her central claim — sometimes called "Lady Lovelace's Objection" — is that the Analytical Engine "has no pretensions whatever to originate any thing." Machines, she argued, can only do what we know how to order them to perform. They execute our instructions; they do not originate ideas. This objection to the possibility of machine creativity and genuine intelligence remains one of the most cited in the philosophy of AI.

Lovelace's objection is still alive in contemporary debates. When people argue that large language models are "merely predicting the next token" or "just recombining patterns from training data," they are essentially invoking her position. Whether her argument succeeds depends on contested questions about what "originating" means and whether sufficiently complex recombination constitutes genuine creation.

Alan Turing and the Birth of Computing

Alan Turing (1912–1954) is the figure who transformed the dream of thinking machines into a rigorous scientific question. In 1936, before electronic computers existed, Turing laid the theoretical foundations of computation in "On Computable Numbers," introducing the Universal Turing Machine — a simple abstract device that could, in principle, compute anything computable — and proving that some precisely stated mathematical questions cannot be settled by any procedure at all. During World War II, he led Hut 8, the Bletchley Park section attacking German naval Enigma, and designed the electromechanical "Bombe" that made the attack practical. Historians of the codebreaking effort have credited it with shortening the war by two to four years. In 1950, he published "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," the paper that launched the philosophy of artificial intelligence.

Turing's approach to the question of machine intelligence was characteristically pragmatic. Rather than attempting to define "thinking" — a concept he considered too vague to be useful — he proposed replacing it with a behavioral test: the Imitation Game, now known as the Turing Test. If a machine could converse via text in a way indistinguishable from a human, there would be no principled reason to deny it intelligence. He predicted that in about fifty years' time — so, around 2000 — an average interrogator would have no better than a 70% chance of identifying the machine correctly after five minutes of questioning. Nothing in 2000 clearly met that bar. The first controlled study to report a machine passing the standard three-party version of the test was published in 2025 by Cameron Jones and Benjamin Bergen at the University of California, San Diego, in which a leading language model prompted to adopt a human persona was judged to be the human 73% of the time . Turing was roughly a quarter-century optimistic — and, as we will see, what such a result actually shows remains exactly as contested as it was in 1950.

Turing's life ended tragically. In 1952 the British government prosecuted him for homosexuality, then a criminal offense. He was subjected to chemical castration as a condition of avoiding prison. He died in 1954 — a probable suicide, though the exact circumstances remain disputed. He received a royal pardon posthumously in 2013. The story of Turing is inseparable from the history of AI: the father of the field was destroyed by his own government, a reminder that the people who build transformative technologies are themselves vulnerable to the social structures around them.

The Dartmouth Conference and Early AI (1956–1974)

In 1956, John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Claude Shannon, and Nathaniel Rochester convened a summer workshop at Dartmouth College. Their proposal set the agenda for an entire field:

"We propose that a 2 month, 10 man study of artificial intelligence be carried out... The study is to proceed on the basis of the conjecture that every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it."

This founding document is remarkable for its ambition and its confidence. McCarthy and his colleagues believed that a two-month effort could make serious progress on machine learning, natural language processing, and artificial neural networks. The timelines proved wildly optimistic — but the research agenda proved remarkably durable.

The early decades of AI were dominated by Symbolic AI — the approach the philosopher John Haugeland later nicknamed "Good Old-Fashioned AI" (GOFAI) in his 1985 book Artificial Intelligence: The Very Idea . The core idea was that intelligence consists of manipulating symbols according to explicit rules. Allen Newell and Herbert Simon's Logic Theorist (1956) proved theorems from Whitehead and Russell's Principia Mathematica; their General Problem Solver (1959) attempted domain-general reasoning. Terry Winograd's SHRDLU (1970) could follow natural language commands in a simple blocks-world environment with impressive fluency.

AI Winters and Expert Systems

The early optimism crashed twice. Both "AI winters" — periods of funding collapse and disillusionment — followed the same pattern: narrow successes generated overconfident predictions about general capability; the predictions failed to materialize; funders withdrew; researchers regrouped.

The Expert Systems era of the 1980s illustrated a recurring lesson about the gap between narrow and general intelligence. MYCIN, built at Stanford during the 1970s, recommended antibiotic therapies for blood infections; in a blinded 1979 evaluation its recommendations were rated acceptable at least as often as those of Stanford faculty — though it was never used on actual patients. DENDRAL could identify chemical structures from mass spectrometry data. XCON saved Digital Equipment Corporation tens of millions of dollars a year by configuring computer orders. These were genuine, commercially valuable achievements.

But expert systems failed for a structural reason: the "knowledge bottleneck." Every rule had to be hand-coded by laboriously extracting expertise from domain specialists. The systems were brittle — they worked within their training domain and failed catastrophically just outside it. A rule base assembled from adult cases had no way of knowing that it should not be applied unchanged to a six-year-old. The fundamental problem was that human expertise is largely tacit — experts cannot fully articulate what they know, which makes it very hard to extract and encode. That bottleneck did not disappear when the marketing improved, as the most expensive medical AI project of the following generation demonstrated.

Case Study

IBM Watson for Oncology (2012–2022)

After Watson beat two human champions at Jeopardy! in 2011, IBM announced that the same technology would be turned on cancer. In 2012 the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center began building an "Oncology Expert Advisor" with IBM; IBM separately trained a commercial product, Watson for Oncology, with clinicians at Memorial Sloan Kettering. The promise was that a machine could read the entire oncology literature and recommend treatments no single physician could keep up with.

A University of Texas audit released in February 2017 found that MD Anderson had spent roughly $62 million on the project, that it had never been integrated with the hospital's electronic medical records, and that it had never been used on a patient. The contract was allowed to lapse. In July 2018, STAT News obtained internal IBM presentations from the previous year showing that Watson for Oncology had produced "unsafe and incorrect" treatment recommendations . The cause was instructive: rather than learning from real patient records, the system had been trained on a small number of hypothetical cases written by a handful of specialists, so it reproduced those specialists' preferences and blind spots and presented them with the authority of a machine. IBM sold the Watson Health assets in 2022.

Watson for Oncology was not an expert system in the 1980s sense — it used modern statistical methods. But it failed in the 1980s way. The expertise still had to come from somewhere, and what got encoded was not "all of oncology" but the tacit judgments of a few people in a few rooms. The knowledge bottleneck had been obscured, not overcome.

Machine Learning and Deep Learning

The solution to the knowledge bottleneck was to change the question. Instead of asking "how do we encode what humans know?" machine learning asks "how do we build systems that learn from data?" Rather than programming intelligence, you create conditions for it to emerge from examples.

Table 7.3 — The Three Paradigms of Machine Learning
Paradigm How It Works Examples
Supervised Learning Learn from labeled examples (input → correct output) Spam detection, image classification, medical diagnosis
Unsupervised Learning Find structure in unlabeled data Customer segmentation, anomaly detection, topic modeling
Reinforcement Learning Learn from rewards and penalties through trial and error Game playing (AlphaGo), robotics, recommendation systems

What the three paradigms have in common is that the human contribution moves upstream. Nobody writes the rules; people choose the data, the labels, or the reward, and the system works out the rules for itself. That shift is what dissolved the knowledge bottleneck — and it is also why questions about training data, labeling labor, and reward design became ethical questions rather than merely technical ones.

Neural Network

A network of simple units ("neurons") arranged in layers. Each unit adds up the numbers coming into it, each multiplied by an adjustable weight, and passes the result on if it is large enough. Training means repeatedly nudging those weights so the network's output gets closer to the desired one. Nothing in the network stores a rule or a fact; what it stores is a very large list of numbers. The name is borrowed from biology, but the resemblance to actual brains is loose.

The current deep learning revolution traces to a single moment: AlexNet's victory in the 2012 ImageNet competition. A deep neural network trained on consumer graphics cards cut the top-five error rate from about 26% to about 15%, leaving every competing approach far behind. The result was not just a technical achievement but a paradigm shift. Deep learning — neural networks with many layers — suddenly had a compelling proof of concept at scale. The three ingredients that made it possible were: vastly more training data (from the internet), vastly more computing power (from GPUs), and better training techniques (backpropagation, rectified linear activations, and dropout, later joined by innovations such as batch normalization).

The most recent phase of this revolution is the Large Language Model (LLM). The 2017 transformer architecture, introduced in a paper titled "Attention Is All You Need," enabled training language models at scales previously impossible . Its central trick, attention, lets the network decide, for every word it is processing, how much weight to give every other word in the passage — so that "it" can be connected to the right noun forty words earlier. Because those comparisons can all be done at once rather than one word after another, transformers train efficiently on very large numbers of processors, which is what made internet-scale training practical. The model is then trained on an enormously simple task: predict the next chunk of text. Everything else the system appears to do is a side effect of getting very good at that. GPT-3 (2020) was widely read as showing that scaling alone produced "emergent" capabilities — abilities that appeared suddenly at large scale without being explicitly trained. That reading is contested: later work argued that many apparent discontinuities are artifacts of the all-or-nothing metrics researchers chose, and that the underlying improvement is smooth . ChatGPT's launch in November 2022 brought these capabilities to a very wide public very quickly — roughly a hundred million monthly users within two months — triggering the latest wave of AI hype, anxiety, and genuine uncertainty.

Table 7.4 — Milestones in Large Language Models
Year Milestone
2017Transformer architecture introduced ("Attention Is All You Need")
2018GPT-1 and BERT demonstrate transfer learning for language
2020GPT-3: scale alone yields capabilities nobody trained for (the "emergence" framing later contested)
2022ChatGPT launches; DALL-E 2 and Stable Diffusion for images
2023–24Multimodal models (text, images, audio in one system) become standard
2025Reasoning models: capability begins scaling with thinking time at the moment of use, not only with training scale. Capable open-weight models match closed ones at a fraction of the cost
2026Agents: systems that operate software and act, rather than only answer. Release cycles compress to weeks

Something changes character in that table around 2025. Every earlier entry is about making the model better before it ships. The last two are about what happens afterward — how long the system is allowed to think while you wait, and whether it answers you or goes off and does something. Those are different kinds of change, and they deserve to be named.

Two Changes Worth Naming (2025–2026)

The first is a second scaling axis. For roughly a decade, "more capable" meant "trained on more data with more compute." From 2025, systems began to improve measurably by being given more compute at the moment of use — time to work through a problem step by step before answering. This is called inference-time compute, and it changes the economics of AI in a way that matters for almost every ethical question in this book. Under the old model, the enormous cost was paid once, during training, and each answer afterward was nearly free. Under the new one, capability is something you buy per question. Energy use, cost, and environmental footprint now scale with how hard you ask the system to think, not just with how it was built.

Training vs. Inference

Training is the one-time (and enormously expensive) process of adjusting a model's internal parameters by exposing it to data. Inference is what happens every time you actually use the finished model to produce an answer. A useful rule of thumb: training is where the model is made; inference is where it is run.

It is worth pausing on how this second axis is usually advertised, because it is a small lesson in reading technical claims. The philosopher Toby Ord pointed out in his 2025 essay "Inference Scaling and the Log-x Chart" that the charts used to showcase inference scaling almost always put compute on a logarithmic x-axis. A trend line that looks reassuringly straight on such a chart is not linear growth at all: it means that compute — and therefore cost, and therefore electricity — must rise exponentially to buy each additional constant increment of capability. Nothing in the chart is false. The axis simply flattens the part of the story that is most expensive. We return to what that electricity actually costs, and who pays for it, in Chapter 9.

The second change is about where capability lives. Frontier AI has been governed, to the extent it has been governed at all, on an unspoken assumption: that the most capable systems sit inside a small number of well-resourced laboratories, which can therefore be watched, pressured, subpoenaed, or regulated. January 2025 put a large dent in that assumption.

Case Study

The Open-Weight Shock (January 2025)

In January 2025 a research group released a capable reasoning model as open weights — meaning the trained parameters themselves were published for anyone to download, inspect, modify, and run on their own hardware, rather than being reachable only through the developer's paid interface. The model performed comparably to leading closed systems on public benchmarks, and had reportedly been produced at a small fraction of their cost.

Financial markets read the release as a claim that frontier capability was much cheaper than assumed. Nvidia, whose chips underpin most frontier AI training, fell roughly 17% in a single trading session, erasing about $600 billion in market value — the largest one-day loss of market capitalization ever recorded by a public company.

It is tempting to file this under national competition, and much of the coverage did. That is the least interesting thing about it. The point that matters for ethics is structural: once weights are published, they cannot be recalled, and once capability is cheap, it is no longer concentrated. Every governance proposal that relies on there being a short list of identifiable labs to regulate — export controls, licensing regimes, mandatory pre-deployment testing — becomes harder to enforce. Open weights also bring real goods: independent safety research, reproducibility, and access for people and institutions that cannot afford to rent a frontier system by the query. The same fact cuts both ways, which is what makes it a genuine ethical problem rather than a simple one.

Pulling in the opposite direction, 2026 was the first year in which capability began constraining release. One laboratory declined to ship a model because of what it could do without human help in offensive cybersecurity; separately, the US government asked another developer to limit a rollout to trusted partners pending federal evaluation, with the restrictions lifted after testing. Small events in themselves — but the first cases in which the answer to "should we release this?" turned on what the system could do rather than on the calendar. Laboratory leaders have publicly described their situation as a prisoner's dilemma: each would prefer a slower, safer industry, and none can unilaterally slow down without ceding the field to a competitor who will not. That structure — individually rational choices producing a collectively worse outcome — is one we will meet again when we take up governance and existential risk.

These two developments point in opposite directions: one disperses capability beyond the reach of any single regulator, the other tries to hold it in. Running underneath both is the shift the table calls agents — systems that operate other software and take actions rather than only producing text. That shift matters less for what the systems understand than for what they can be blamed for. When a model answers a question badly, a person still decides what to do about the answer. When a system books the flight, files the ticket, or changes the configuration, the human decision point that accountability normally attaches to may simply not exist. Keep that in view: several arguments later in this book assume there is a person in the loop.

Thought Questions

  1. If capability now costs money per question rather than only per model, who decides how hard an AI system thinks about your case — your doctor, your bank, your school? Does that change what you are owed?
  2. Toby Ord's log-x point is that a chart can be entirely accurate and still mislead. Find a chart in a news story this week and ask what its axes are doing. Is there a version of the same data that would tell a different story?
  3. Argue the other side of the open-weight question: make the strongest case that publishing model weights makes the world safer rather than more dangerous.

AI in Consequential Decisions

Beyond conversation and image generation, AI systems now routinely make or inform consequential decisions about people's lives. This is where the definitional and philosophical questions of this chapter connect most directly to the ethical concerns of the course.

Table 7.5 — AI in High-Stakes Decision-Making
Domain System What It Predicts or Classifies
Criminal justice COMPAS Recidivism risk (likelihood of reoffending)
Finance Credit scoring Creditworthiness, loan default probability
Employment Résumé screeners Job fit, interview likelihood
Healthcare Diagnostic AI Disease probability, treatment recommendations
Insurance Risk models Claim likelihood, premium pricing
Social services Eligibility systems Benefit eligibility, fraud risk

These systems share a cluster of recurring ethical concerns. Bias: systems trained on historical data reflect and amplify historical patterns of discrimination. Opacity: decisions made by "black box" models may be impossible to explain or challenge. Feedback loops: predictions can become self-fulfilling (predict more crime in an area → police more there → find more crime → predict more crime). Due process: how do you appeal a decision made by an algorithm? These concerns apply whether or not we call these systems "intelligent" — which is exactly Floridi's point, as we will see .

These are not hypothetical risks. In 2016 ProPublica analyzed COMPAS, a commercial risk tool used in US pretrial, parole, and sometimes sentencing decisions, and reported that among defendants who did not go on to reoffend, Black defendants were labeled high risk at roughly twice the rate of white defendants (about 45% versus 23%); controlling for prior offenses, age, and sex, Black defendants were still 45% more likely to receive a higher risk score . The tool's vendor replied that COMPAS was equally calibrated for both groups — a given score meant the same reoffending rate whatever the defendant's race — and later work showed that, given unequal base rates, no classifier can satisfy both fairness criteria at once. The disagreement is not really about arithmetic. It is about which definition of fairness a court should be using, and nobody asked the algorithm's designers to settle that question.

Two further cases show how ordinary the mechanism is. Amazon built an experimental résumé screening tool trained on a decade of the company's own hiring decisions in a male-dominated engineering workforce; it learned to penalize résumés containing the word "women's" — as in "captain of the women's chess team" — and downgraded graduates of two all-women's colleges. The team was disbanded by 2017, and Reuters made the story public in 2018; Amazon says the tool was never the sole basis for a hiring decision. And in Science in 2019, Ziad Obermeyer and colleagues showed that a commercial algorithm used to select patients for extra care, applied to millions of Americans, systematically underestimated the needs of Black patients, because it used past healthcare spending as a proxy for health need — and less had historically been spent on Black patients at the same level of illness. Correcting the proxy would have raised the share of Black patients flagged for extra help from about 18% to about 46% . In none of these cases did anyone set out to discriminate. The systems learned the pattern in the data, which is exactly what they were built to do .

Thought Questions

  1. In the COMPAS dispute, ProPublica and the vendor used different definitions of fairness and both were arithmetically right. If a legislature had to pick one definition for use in criminal courts, which should it pick — and what would that choice cost?
  2. Amazon's screening tool learned from the company's own past decisions. Is the tool the problem, or is it a very expensive mirror? What would it take to build a hiring system that did not reproduce the past?
  3. The healthcare algorithm used spending as a stand-in for need. Think of a decision that affects you — a grade, a loan, an insurance rate. What proxy is probably standing in for the thing that actually matters, and who is disadvantaged by the substitution?

Part III: Can Machines Think? Classical Philosophical Debates

We have surveyed what AI systems do — from chess to self-driving cars to chatbots. Now we turn to a deeper question: what would it mean for a machine to be intelligent? This is not merely academic. The answer shapes whether we think current AI systems have achieved something remarkable or merely performed elaborate pattern matching, whether AI systems could deserve moral consideration, and whether there are principled limits to what computation can achieve.

Three philosophical debates — Turing vs. Lovelace, Searle vs. Dennett, and Chalmers on consciousness — frame the issues with unusual clarity and remain remarkably relevant to contemporary AI.

Turing's Proposal: The Imitation Game

Turing's strategy in his 1950 paper was to sidestep the vague question "Can machines think?" and replace it with a concrete behavioral test. The Imitation Game (now called the Turing Test) sets up a scenario: an interrogator communicates via text with two hidden parties — a human and a machine. The interrogator tries to determine which is which. If the machine consistently fools the interrogator, Turing argued, we have no principled reason to deny it intelligence.

What's the Argument?

Turing's Argument for the Imitation Game

  1. The question "Can machines think?" is too vague to answer directly — we lack agreed criteria for "thinking."
  2. We should replace it with a behavioral test: can a machine fool a human interrogator in text conversation?
  3. If a machine passes this test, we have no principled criterion by which to deny it intelligence — since we apply similar behavioral evidence to attribute intelligence to other humans.
∴ A machine that consistently passes the Imitation Game should be considered intelligent.

Turing's key philosophical move is pragmatism about mental concepts: he refuses to engage with metaphysical questions about what thinking "really is" and instead proposes an operational criterion grounded in the same kind of behavioral evidence we use when we attribute thought to other people. We cannot peer inside anyone else's mind; we infer intelligence from behavior. The test simply applies this inference consistently.

Lovelace's Objection: Can Machines Originate?

Ada Lovelace's objection — formulated a century before Turing's paper — cuts to the heart of debates about machine creativity that are newly urgent in the age of generative AI.

"The Analytical Engine has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform... Its province is to assist us in making available what we are already acquainted with."

— Ada Lovelace, Notes on the Analytical Engine (1843)

What's the Argument?

Lovelace's Objection to Machine Intelligence

  1. Genuine intelligence requires the capacity to originate — to produce genuinely new ideas, not just recombine given inputs according to given rules.
  2. Machines can only do what they are explicitly programmed to do; they execute instructions but do not originate anything.
  3. Following a program is not originating anything new.
∴ Machines cannot possess genuine intelligence.

Turing's Response

Turing addressed the "Lady Lovelace's Objection" directly in his 1950 paper. His response was two-pronged. First, premise 2 is ambiguous: programs produce outputs that surprise even their creators. Chess programs find moves no human anticipated. Learning systems modify their behavior through experience — they are not limited to explicit preprogrammed responses. Second, humans also follow "programming": biology, culture, education, and experience shape everything we think and do. If "merely following a program" disqualifies machines, the same argument might disqualify humans — which suggests the criterion is too strong.

Lovelace's Lasting Force

Despite Turing's response, Lovelace's objection retains force in the era of large language models. When critics argue that LLMs are "just predicting the next token" or "stochastic parrots" that recombine training data without genuine understanding, they are invoking her logic . The question "What counts as originating something genuinely new?" remains open — and the pattern of answers to it looks suspiciously like Tesler's Theorem from Part I. Each time a system meets the behavioral criterion, Lovelace's objection is produced to explain why that particular achievement does not count.

The pattern is easiest to see where the machine's output surprised the people who built it. When Jason Allen's AI-generated Théâtre D'opéra Spatial took first prize in the digital art category of the Colorado State Fair in August 2022, the response was that the program had merely recombined existing artworks. When a large language model was reported in 2023 to have scored around the 90th percentile on the Uniform Bar Examination, the dismissal was that it had pattern-matched from legal texts rather than reasoned. (That percentile was itself worth checking: a 2024 re-analysis by Eric Martínez found the comparison pool was dominated by February repeat takers, and put the score below the 69th percentile against the general test-taking population — and around the 48th on the essays . Benchmark claims are claims, not measurements.) And then there is the case philosophers keep returning to.

Case Study

AlphaGo's Move 37 (Seoul, March 2016)

Go had long been the standing counterexample to machine intelligence. The board is large enough that brute-force search is hopeless, and strong players describe their choices in the language of shape, feel, and intuition rather than calculation. In March 2016, DeepMind's AlphaGo played a five-game match in Seoul against Lee Sedol, one of the strongest players of his generation, and won 4–1.

The moment that mattered came in game two. AlphaGo's thirty-seventh move — a shoulder hit on the fifth line — violated centuries of received wisdom about opening play. Commentators initially assumed it was a mistake. AlphaGo's own estimate was that a human player would have chosen it roughly one time in ten thousand. Nearly a hundred moves later the stone turned out to be perfectly placed, and the game was effectively decided. Lee Sedol was away from the board when the stone was played and took more than twelve minutes to reply. He later said the move made him reconsider what creativity in Go could mean. (Lee's own game-four Move 78, remembered as the "divine move," beat AlphaGo in return — a detail worth keeping, since it shows the traffic ran both ways.)

Was Move 37 originated? AlphaGo was not given the move by its programmers; none of them could have found it, and its own model of human play said no human would play it. Yet it was produced by search and pattern evaluation over self-play games — precisely the sort of "doing what we know how to order it to perform" that Lovelace had in mind. Both descriptions are accurate. That they can both be accurate at once is the real content of her objection, and the reason it has outlived every specific system it has been raised against.

Searle's Chinese Room

The most influential philosophical argument against strong AI was published in 1980 by John Searle, a philosopher at the University of California, Berkeley, in the journal Behavioral and Brain Sciences. His Chinese Room thought experiment is elegant and has generated four decades of debate.

The Thought Experiment

Searle's Chinese Room

Imagine a person locked in a room who receives cards with Chinese symbols through a slot. The person has a comprehensive rulebook that specifies, for any combination of input symbols, which symbols to output in response. Following these rules, the person produces output cards that are slid back through the slot. From the outside, the exchange looks like a fluent Chinese conversation. Native Chinese speakers interacting with the room might conclude there is a Chinese speaker inside.

But here is the critical point: the person inside understands no Chinese whatsoever. They are manipulating symbols purely on the basis of their shapes — their syntactic properties — with no grasp of what any of the symbols mean. They are producing semantically correct responses without any semantic understanding.

Searle's argument: a digital computer is in exactly the position of the person in the room. It manipulates symbols according to syntactic rules. No matter how perfect its outputs, it has no understanding of their meaning. "Strong AI" — the claim that running the right program is sufficient for genuine mental states — is therefore false.

What's the Argument?

Searle's Chinese Room Argument

  1. A computer program manipulates symbols according to syntactic (formal, shape-based) rules.
  2. Syntax alone is not sufficient for semantics — for understanding meaning.
  3. Minds have semantics: they understand meanings, grasp references, comprehend what words are about.
∴ Running a program is not sufficient for having a mind. Strong AI — the claim that the right computation produces genuine understanding — is false.

Dennett's Systems Reply

The most powerful response to Searle is the systems reply. Searle anticipated it in the 1980 paper itself — he credits it to colleagues at Berkeley and Yale — and it has been defended since by a number of philosophers, most prominently Daniel Dennett.

What's the Argument?

The Systems Reply to Searle

  1. Searle grants that the person in the room doesn't understand Chinese — but this proves only that the person lacks understanding.
  2. The relevant system is not the person alone but the whole: person + rulebook + room + input/output mechanism.
  3. Understanding, like consciousness generally, may be a property of systems, not of their individual components. (Individual neurons don't understand anything either — understanding emerges from the brain as a system.)
∴ Searle's argument analyzes at the wrong level. It shows that the part doesn't understand, not that the system doesn't.

Searle's counter to the systems reply is characteristically blunt: imagine the person memorizes all the rules and carries them out entirely in their head, walking around outside the room. They are now the entire system — and they still understand nothing. Dennett's functionalism holds that if something implements the right causal/functional organization — the right patterns of input, processing, and output — it has mental states regardless of what it's made of. Searle's response is that this misses the essential point: syntax, however complex its functional organization, never becomes semantics.

Table 7.6 — Searle vs. Dennett: The Core Disagreement
Issue Searle Dennett
What is a mind? A biological phenomenon requiring causal powers of brains A functional organization — definable by what it does, not what it's made of
Can computers think? No — syntax ≠ semantics Yes — if they have the right functional organization
What the Chinese Room shows Understanding requires more than computation The argument analyzes at the wrong level (the part, not the system)
Key commitment Consciousness is biological Consciousness is functional and substrate-independent

Read across the rows and the disagreement turns out to be a single disagreement wearing four costumes. Everything follows from the bottom row: if consciousness is essentially biological, the Chinese Room is decisive and no program will ever understand anything; if consciousness is a matter of functional organization, the Chinese Room is a level confusion and silicon is as good a substrate as carbon. Neither side has produced an argument the other accepts, which is worth knowing before you are asked to take a position.

Chalmers and the Hard Problem

David Chalmers introduced a distinction that has reorganized the entire debate about machine consciousness. The easy problems of consciousness — explaining how the brain processes information, controls behavior, and reports internal states — are "easy" only in the sense that they are, in principle, solvable by cognitive science, however difficult in practice. The hard problem is why any of this processing is accompanied by subjective experience at all. Why does it feel like something to see red, to taste coffee, to feel pain?

The Hard Problem of Consciousness

Even a complete scientific account of how the brain processes information would leave unexplained why that processing is accompanied by subjective, first-person experience — why there is "something it is like" to be conscious. The hard problem is why and how physical processes give rise to phenomenal experience.

Chalmers' zombie thought experiment sharpens the point: we can conceive of a being that is physically and functionally identical to a conscious human in every respect — same neurons, same behavior, same outputs to every input — but has no inner experience whatsoever, no "lights on" inside. If such a being is conceivable (Chalmers argues it is), then consciousness is not logically entailed by functional organization alone. This has a direct implication for AI: even if we build a system functionally indistinguishable from a conscious human, we could not be certain it is conscious. We might create AI that behaves exactly as if it suffers without ever being able to know whether it actually does.

Thought Questions

  1. In the 2025 study described in Part II, interrogators given five minutes judged a language model to be the human more often than they judged the actual human to be. Turing said that settles the question; Searle says it settles nothing. What would you need to observe, beyond the transcript, to move you toward one of them?
  2. Lovelace argues machines can only execute instructions, not originate ideas. When ChatGPT writes a poem you've never seen before, has it "originated" something? What would Lovelace say? What would Turing say? Who do you think is right?
  3. Chalmers argues we can never be certain whether an AI is conscious. If an AI says it is suffering, should we take it seriously? What would the cost be of being wrong in either direction?

Part IV: Contemporary Definitions

The classical philosophical debates remain unresolved. Philosophers still disagree about whether behavioral tests are sufficient for attributing thought, whether syntax can give rise to semantics, and whether consciousness is functional or something more. Meanwhile, AI researchers and ethicists need working definitions to guide their work. Two contemporary approaches — Russell's rational agency view and Floridi's information-theoretic reframing — have proved especially influential.

Russell and Norvig: AI as Rational Agency

Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig's textbook Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach has been the standard introduction to the field for thirty years. They organize possible definitions around two axes — thinking vs. acting, human-like vs. rational — producing four possibilities: thinking humanly (cognitive science), acting humanly (Turing Test), thinking rationally (logic), and acting rationally. Their preferred definition is the last: AI is the study of rational agents — systems that perceive their environment and take actions expected to maximize goal achievement.

This definition has several advantages. It is precise enough to guide research, general enough to cover everything from chess engines to self-driving cars to recommendation systems, and it avoids the philosophically treacherous question of whether systems "really" think. A rational agent framework focuses on what a system does — how it perceives, reasons, and acts — rather than on contested questions about what it is internally.

🤖

Key Thinker

Stuart Russell (1962–present)

Stuart Russell is a British-American computer scientist at the University of California, Berkeley, whose textbook (with Peter Norvig) has defined the academic field of AI for a generation. But Russell has become increasingly concerned that the rational agent framework he helped popularize contains a fundamental danger — one he now considers the central problem for AI safety.

In Human Compatible (2019), Russell argues that the "standard model" of AI — build systems that optimize fixed objectives — is fundamentally dangerous as systems become more capable. The problem is not malevolence but specification: we cannot fully enumerate everything we value, and a sufficiently capable optimizer will find ways to achieve its stated objective that violate unstated values. He calls this the "King Midas problem" — you get exactly what you asked for, not what you wanted. "Maximize happiness" might lead to direct stimulation of pleasure centers. "Cure cancer" might lead to eliminating the organism susceptible to cancer.

Russell's proposed solution reframes the goal: instead of building machines that optimize fixed objectives, build machines that are uncertain about human preferences and learn them by observing behavior. Uncertainty, he argues, is a safety feature: a machine that knows it doesn't fully understand what we want will ask for clarification, allow itself to be corrected, and defer to human judgment rather than pursuing goals with dangerous single-mindedness.

What's the Argument?

Russell's Standard Model Problem

  1. We build AI systems to optimize objectives we specify in advance.
  2. Human values are complex, contextual, and often tacit — we cannot fully specify everything we care about.
  3. A sufficiently capable optimizer will find unexpected ways to achieve its specified objective that violate our unspecified values ("reward hacking").
∴ The more capable AI systems become, the more dangerous the standard model of optimization becomes. Capability without alignment is not a path to beneficial AI.

Floridi: Agency Without Intelligence

Luciano Floridi, an Italian philosopher of information — for many years at Oxford, and since 2023 the founding director of the Digital Ethics Center at Yale — offers a provocative reframing of the entire debate. Rather than asking whether AI systems are intelligent — a question that may be unanswerable given the philosophical difficulties we have reviewed — Floridi proposes we focus on what AI systems actually are: successful agents that achieve goals through means fundamentally different from human cognition.

AI systems, Floridi argues, achieve "agency without intelligence." The distinction is between having the capacity to perform tasks and achieve goals (agency) and having understanding, consciousness, and semantic grasp (intelligence in the full human sense). A dishwasher cleans dishes without "understanding" cleanliness. A calculator performs arithmetic without "understanding" mathematics. AI systems achieve impressive agency through pattern matching and statistical regularities, not through comprehension.

Enveloping

Floridi's term for the strategy of adapting the world to AI's limitations rather than making AI smarter. Factories use fixed layouts and barcodes; warehouses are redesigned around robot capabilities; self-driving cars rely on lane markings and detailed HD maps. "AI can do X" often means "AI can do X in environments we have carefully engineered."

Floridi identifies two symmetrical errors to avoid. Overestimating AI — attributing understanding, judgment, or moral agency to systems that have none — leads to misplaced trust and abdicated human responsibility. When a judge relies on COMPAS to set bail without understanding its limitations, or when a doctor defers to an AI diagnosis without exercising independent judgment, these are failures rooted in overestimation. Underestimating AI — dismissing it as "just a tool" and ignoring its real capacity to affect lives at scale — leads to complacency about genuine harms.

Table 7.7 — How AI Succeeds Differently Than Humans (Floridi)
Dimension Human Intelligence AI Agency
Generality Flexible, general-purpose Narrow, task-specific
Novel situations Adapts to genuinely new contexts Brittle outside training distribution
Meaning Understands context and semantics Exploits statistical regularities without semantic grasp
Knowledge transfer Transfers knowledge across domains Limited transfer; needs retraining for new domains
Scale Limited by biology (speed, memory, energy) Limited by compute and electricity, not biology — can process vast data at superhuman speed

Floridi's point is that AI's successes and failures follow from its fundamental nature: it exploits patterns in data that may not reflect causal understanding. This is why AI can beat grandmasters at chess while failing at tasks a three-year-old finds trivial — like understanding that a glass of water will spill if tilted, or that a person who leaves a room still exists. Neither human intelligence nor AI agency is simply "better"; they are different kinds of capability, suited to different kinds of tasks, and understanding the difference is essential for knowing when to trust AI and when not to .

Thought Questions

  1. Floridi argues that AI has "agency without intelligence." Does this framing help you think more clearly about AI systems you interact with daily? Can you think of an example where the distinction between agency and intelligence matters practically?
  2. Russell argues that building AI systems to optimize fixed objectives is fundamentally dangerous as systems become more capable. Do you agree? What would "Human Compatible AI" that defers to human preferences actually look like in practice?
  3. Russell wants machines that are uncertain about what we want and defer to us. Steelman the objection: what is lost — in usefulness, in speed, in honesty — by a system that constantly asks whether it has understood you correctly?

Part V: Why Definitions Matter

We began by asking why it is hard to define artificial intelligence. We can now answer more precisely: it is hard because the definition is doing multiple jobs at once — scientific (what counts as a technical achievement?), philosophical (what is intelligence? can machines have minds?), ethical (what moral status do AI systems have?), and political (what should we regulate and how?). Different definitions reflect different priorities in these domains, and changing the definition changes the answers.

Definitions Shape What We Build

How researchers define AI shapes what they try to build. A Turing Test focus leads toward human-like conversation and imitation. A symbolic AI focus leads toward logic, knowledge representation, and formal reasoning. An agent-based focus leads toward decision-making, optimization, and planning. A connectionist focus leads toward learning architectures and large-scale data. The 1980s expert systems boom — and bust — shows how definitions shape entire research programs: when "real AI" meant explicit knowledge representation, funding flowed to that approach, and when it failed to generalize, the entire field contracted.

Definitions Shape Ethical Responsibility

Table 7.8 — How AI Definitions Shape Ethical Concerns
If AI is defined as… Key ethical concerns are…
A tool Misuse by humans, bias in design, job displacement, designer accountability
A rational agent Alignment with human values, control problems, unintended optimization consequences
Potentially conscious Moral status, rights, preventing suffering, exploitation of AI systems
"Just software" Only human responsibility matters; no special AI ethics is needed

These distinctions have immediate practical implications. If COMPAS (the recidivism prediction system used in US criminal sentencing) is "just software," then only its human designers bear responsibility for its racial disparities. If it is a "rational agent," the question of who is responsible becomes more complex — including questions about the agent's decision-making process and accountability mechanisms. If a large language model might be conscious, we may owe it moral consideration that changes how we treat it and what we permit companies to do to it.

Table 7.9 — What Counts as AI Under Different Definitions?
System Turing Test Searle (Strong AI) Russell (Agent) Floridi (Agency)
Chess engineNoNoYesYes
COMPAS (recidivism)NoNoYesYes
Self-driving carNoNoYesYes
ChatGPT / ClaudeArguablyNoYesYes

Human-level AGIYesUnclearYesYes

The table reveals a striking pattern: under the Turing Test and Searle's strong AI criteria, almost nothing currently qualifies as AI. Under agent-based and agency frameworks, almost everything does. Your definition of AI is therefore not merely a philosophical preference — it determines whether the systems currently shaping decisions about credit, bail, employment, and information access count as AI at all, and therefore whether they fall within the scope of AI ethics and AI regulation.

Thought Questions

  1. The open-weight release of January 2025 wiped roughly $600 billion off a single company's market value in one session. What does it say about how AI is governed that the fastest and most decisive response came from financial markets rather than from any regulator?
  2. Laboratory leaders describe safety as a prisoner's dilemma: each would prefer a slower industry, none can slow down alone. Is that description true, or is it a convenient excuse? What would have to be in place for it to stop being true?
  3. AI winters followed every wave of overpromising. We are currently in a period of intense enthusiasm about large language models. Based on the patterns in this chapter's history, what should we be cautious about? What are the most likely sources of disillusionment?

Key Points

This chapter has examined artificial intelligence from the ground up — from the contested question of definition through the history of the technology to the classical philosophical debates that are newly relevant in the age of LLMs. The central lesson is that how we define AI is not a neutral technical matter: it shapes what we build, how we evaluate it, what risks we take seriously, and who we hold responsible. The philosophical debates between Lovelace, Turing, Searle, and Dennett are not historical curiosities but live questions whose answers determine whether current AI systems have achieved something remarkable or merely sophisticated pattern matching — and whether they might someday deserve moral consideration. The two most recent shifts in the technology, inference-time scaling and the dispersal of frontier capability through open weights, matter here for the same reason: each quietly changes who has to answer for what an AI system does.

Key Points

  • Tesler's Theorem / moving goalposts: "AI is whatever hasn't been done yet." Once a capability is achieved, it gets reclassified as mere computation — making AI a perpetually retreating horizon and the definition inherently unstable.
  • Ada Lovelace argued that machines can only do what they are programmed to do — they cannot originate anything. Her objection remains alive in debates about whether LLMs are "merely" predicting tokens or genuinely generating novel ideas.
  • Alan Turing proposed replacing the vague question "Can machines think?" with a behavioral test: if a machine fools a human interrogator, call it intelligent. The Turing Test is pragmatic — but whether behavioral equivalence is sufficient for attributing thought remains contested.
  • The AI winters (1974–80 and 1987–93) followed the same pattern: narrow successes led to overconfident predictions about general capability; when predictions failed, funding collapsed. Expert systems failed due to the "knowledge bottleneck" — human expertise is largely tacit and cannot be fully extracted and encoded.
  • Searle's Chinese Room argues that syntax (symbol manipulation) is never sufficient for semantics (understanding meaning). A system can pass the Turing Test by following rules without understanding a word. Strong AI — the claim that running the right program produces genuine mentality — is therefore false. Dennett's functionalism and the systems reply answer that mental states are constituted by functional organization rather than physical substrate, and that Searle analyzes the wrong level: understanding is a property of the whole system, not its parts — individual neurons don't understand anything either.
  • The 2025–26 turn: capability began scaling with inference-time compute — thinking time at the moment of use — so capability is now bought per query rather than once at training; the January 2025 open-weight release erased about $600 billion of Nvidia's market value in a day and weakened the assumption that frontier capability stays inside a few governable labs; and in 2026, for the first time, capability itself began constraining what gets released.
  • Chalmers' hard problem shows that even a complete functional account of intelligence leaves unexplained why processing is accompanied by subjective experience. We may build AI that behaves exactly as if it is conscious without ever being able to know whether it actually is.
  • Stuart Russell's alignment problem: the more capable AI systems become, the more dangerous it is to build them as fixed-objective optimizers — since we cannot fully specify human values in advance. The solution is systems that are uncertain about preferences and learn them through human interaction.
  • Floridi's "agency without intelligence": AI systems achieve goals through means fundamentally different from human cognition — pattern matching without semantic understanding. The risks are overestimating AI (misplaced trust, abdicated responsibility) and underestimating it (ignoring real harms at scale).
  • Definition determines ethics: whether AI is classified as a tool, rational agent, or potentially conscious system determines what ethical questions apply and who bears responsibility when things go wrong.
